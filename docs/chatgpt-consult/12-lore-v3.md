THE TOWER GAME — LORE v3
Version status: Expansion of LORE v2. Powers I–IV remain canonically unchanged except for the two requested additions below. Powers V–VIII are developed to the same miniature-grade standard. The new conflicts and Dragon texts establish political and doctrinal tensions, not additional game mechanics.
Design rule: These are institutions, not nations. Their members may fight under the same banner while disagreeing over doctrine, authority, resources or the legitimacy of their own leadership. Their visual identities should remain readable at miniature scale, even when several armies occupy the same battlefield.
PART I — CHANGES TO POWERS I–IV
Only the following additions are made. All other v2 material remains unchanged.
I. The Iron Fists — additions
Runic Gauntlets: The runic branch uses normal-scale iron gauntlets engraved with small fire-runes and set with two or three dull ember-stone studs; the stones do not shine or flare, and any visible glow remains confined to the engraved lines, because the Order regards the technique as a guarded craft secret rather than a spectacle.
The visual distinction is deliberate: these are not oversized magical weapons covered in glowing ornaments. They are ordinary-looking iron gauntlets whose construction conceals something considerably more dangerous.
Silhouette and cloth clarification: The Monk's gauntlets remain heavy, but the surrounding clothing should read clearly as black cloth with yellow markings and a brown cloak. The cloak is worn, practical and frequently patched; it should not obscure the yellow Order identifiers.
III. The Verdant Warrant — Archer correction
Archers carry heavy bows, not crossbows.
The bow is a substantial weapon with reinforced limbs, a thick string and a draw that requires the archer to set their feet, brace the lower limb and pull with the back rather than simply the arms. At full draw, the shoulder is drawn back, the upper body tightens and the fingers strain against the string. A proper shot leaves the archer's drawing hand swollen and split, the shoulder inflamed and the muscles along the spine aching. Repeated volleys damage joints and tendons; veteran archers often have one shoulder sitting slightly lower than the other.
The weapon is not fast to prepare or comfortable to use. Its purpose is to deliver a heavy, narrow-headed arrow with enough force to penetrate ordinary infantry armour. A Warrant archer is expected to endure the cost of that force personally.
PART II — THE FOUR NEW POWERS
V. THE MERIDIAN OFFICE
“A border is not a line. It is the point at which someone is made to stop.”
A. Institutional portrait
The Meridian Office began as an administrative body responsible for surveying territory, maintaining maps and recording the relationship between settlements, roads, rivers, towers and the Architect infrastructure. Its predecessors did not merely draw maps. They measured distances, elevation, watercourses, population density, soil quality and the practical reach of administrative authority.
After the Silence, the maps remained more useful than the governments that had commissioned them.
The Office survived because every surviving power needed to know where it stood, what it could reach and what it could claim. Its surveyors became negotiators, its boundary officers became armed escorts, and its cartographers learned to defend their measurements with soldiers. In time, the Office began to enforce the borders it recorded.
Its authority rests on the register. A settlement not entered in the register may be taxed twice, supplied never or declared nonexistent when its inhabitants demand protection. A road not recorded as maintained is not considered damaged when a convoy disappears along it. The Office rarely needs to lie. It can simply refuse to recognise the evidence.
Meridian soldiers are trained to understand terrain as a sequence of measurements. Their officers carry instruments even in battle. They mark distances, identify usable ground and record changes to the landscape. Their camps are ordered around straight lines and measured intervals. Tents are aligned, stores counted, and latrines placed at recorded distances from water.
Their food is dry travel bread, salted meat, hard cheese and bitter tea. Fresh provisions are often withheld until a survey party completes its assigned work. A sick officer may be excused from marching but not from signing a revised map.
The camps smell of oiled leather, wet parchment, lampblack, metal instruments and old ink. Surveyors develop calluses across the fingers that hold measuring rods. Repeated use of optical instruments leaves some with headaches, blurred vision and permanent pale marks around the eyes.
The dead are recorded by location, name and recoverable equipment. If a body cannot be found, its last known coordinates are entered. If the coordinates are disputed, the body is considered unlocated until a second survey confirms otherwise.
Among the Office's most common sayings is: “If it cannot be placed, it cannot be defended.”
The Office claims to preserve an accurate picture of the world. Its contradiction is that it increasingly treats anything that cannot be measured as if it has no right to exist.

D. Dragon belief
The Meridian Office does not officially recognise Heaven as a measurable destination. Its records describe Dragons as exiled celestial entities whose movements suggest an origin beyond the known sky. Their exhaustion is considered a persistent condition, not a disease.
The Office has measured tower heights, Dragon resting intervals and the distances between successive perches. It has also recorded their refusal to land on Arcane Towers. None of these measurements explains the creature's relationship with the sky.
Some senior surveyors believe the legendary arcane spire is a reference to a structure capable of crossing a boundary that ordinary instruments cannot detect. Others consider the story an old religious interpretation of an unknown physical event.
The Office does not claim that the Dragons will return. It claims that the question remains open.
E. Field manual excerpt — Boundary Discipline
    A boundary is not established by the man who first places a marker. It is established by the men who remain when someone attempts to remove it.
    Survey before occupation. Measure before construction. Record before destruction.
    Do not accept a local account of distance when a verified instrument is available. Do not accept a local account of ownership when the register states otherwise.
    If a marker is broken, replace it at the recorded coordinate. If the coordinate is disputed, establish a second reference point. If both are disputed, secure the ground until the Office has ruled.
    A village may move. A river may change course. A road may disappear beneath ash. The register must not be permitted to change merely because the world has become inconvenient.
    Report missing surveyors by their last verified position. Do not report them as dead without evidence.
    An unrecorded loss is not a loss the Office can account for.
VI. THE GLEANERS' COMMUNION
“The harvest belongs to the living. The living belong to the harvest.”
A. Institutional portrait
The Gleaners' Communion descends from the agricultural authorities that managed food production, granaries, seed reserves and population provisioning under the Architects. Its predecessors measured harvest yields, soil exhaustion, water availability, storage losses and the number of mouths a district could sustain.
The Silence did not destroy the need for food. It destroyed the central system that had coordinated its distribution.
The Communion survived by taking responsibility for granaries and irrigation works. At first, its members were local custodians and harvest supervisors. They soon became armed authorities. A granary could not be defended by a ledger alone, particularly when the neighbouring settlement had not eaten in six days.
Its doctrine is simple: food must be accounted for before it can be distributed. Every harvest is divided into seed, reserve, tithe, labour ration and emergency allocation. The categories are treated as necessities rather than decisions. The Communion does not describe a withheld ration as a punishment. It calls it a correction to the district's allocation.
Its settlements are built around storage buildings, threshing floors, drying sheds and guarded water channels. The largest granaries resemble fortresses. Their doors are thick, their windows narrow, and their walls marked with the harvest years they have survived.
The Communion's soldiers are not always professional warriors. Many are former field workers, mill hands, irrigation guards and storehouse attendants. Their armour is padded and layered, built to protect against tools, debris and the close violence of defending a granary. Iron harvesting hooks and heavy blades are common equipment.
They eat coarse bread, bean porridge, dried roots, salted vegetables and whatever grain has begun to spoil but remains edible. Senior officials may receive meat and fresh produce, but even their meals are recorded as institutional expenditure. A common ration is measured by weight, not by hunger.
The camps smell of damp grain, sour flour, animal dung, wet straw and smoke from drying fires. Grain dust settles into clothing and lungs. Workers develop cracked hands, swollen joints and chronic coughs. Mould exposure is common in old storage buildings, and the Communion's healers distinguish between those who can still work and those who can no longer lift a sack.
The dead are stripped of usable clothing and tools. In famine districts, bodies may be buried beneath the field they worked, provided the burial does not interfere with the next planting. The Communion does not consider this disrespectful. It calls it returning labour to the soil.
Its most common saying is: “A full granary is a quiet village.”
The institution claims to preserve life by feeding the population. It also decides which population is worth feeding.

D. Dragon belief
The Gleaners' Communion regards Dragons as celestial beings whose exile has left them unable to sustain themselves. Their exhaustion is interpreted as a form of deprivation: they are separated from the place that once nourished them.

The old legend of the arcane spire is treated as a promise of restoration. Some Gleaners believe the return of Dragons would restore a balance lost during the Silence. Others consider that belief dangerously sentimental. A creature that cannot be fed, housed or accounted for is not necessarily a blessing.
The Communion has no proof that Dragons require food, or that the heavens would receive them again.
E. Harvest instruction — The Empty Granary
    The granary is not empty because the people are hungry. It is empty because the harvest has been divided.
    First, preserve the seed. A district that eats its seed has eaten its next year. Second, preserve the reserve. A district without reserve is already in famine, whether it admits it or not. Third, account for the tithe. The tithe is not the remainder after the people have eaten. It is the portion owed before distribution.
    Do not open a storehouse because a crowd has gathered. A crowd is not a measure of need.
    Count the children. Count the workers. Count the infirm. Count the dead. Do not count a missing man twice because two families claim his ration.
    If the harvest fails, the allocation changes. If the allocation fails, the district changes.
    A field can be planted again. A granary can be rebuilt. A population that has consumed its future cannot be restored by regret.
    Close the doors when the last measure is taken.
VII. THE SEPULCHRAL REGISTRY
“The dead do not disappear. They become our responsibility.”
A. Institutional portrait
The Sepulchral Registry began as an administrative authority responsible for deaths, burial records, inheritance, military losses and the identification of remains. Under the Architects, its predecessors maintained records that connected a person's death to property, family, labour obligations and the transfer of institutional responsibility.
After the Silence, those records became more important than ever. Families needed proof of death. Commanders needed casualty counts. Settlements needed to know who had inherited a workshop, who still owed labour and whose body had never been returned.
The Registry survived because every war produces more dead than any institution can afford to ignore.
Its officers are not priests, though they maintain rites and employ mortuary attendants. Their authority comes from the record. A death entered into the Registry can be used to settle inheritance, close debts, release a household from obligations or claim the deceased's equipment. A death not entered remains administratively unresolved.
Its military branches developed from the need to recover bodies, protect burial sites and secure casualty records during campaigns. Registry soldiers carry identification plates, numbered equipment and sealed document cases. Their officers are trained to distinguish between a confirmed death, an unlocated casualty and a body whose identity cannot be established.
The Registry's institutions are cold, orderly places. Their halls contain shelves of sealed records, bone boxes, engraved plates and rolls of names. The floors are washed repeatedly, but the smell of old wax, damp stone, ash, antiseptic herbs and decay remains.
Workers eat black bread, broth, preserved fish and salted meat. Meals are taken at fixed intervals because the dead are not considered a reason to suspend the schedule. Some mortuary attendants develop a habit of eating without speaking. Others refuse to eat near the body rooms.
The Registry's staff suffer from damaged hands, chemical burns, chronic respiratory illness and injuries caused by lifting bodies in armour. The fingers of senior registrars are often stained by ink and preservation compounds. Some develop a permanent tremor from years of working with delicate identification instruments.
The dead are stripped, washed, identified and entered. Those with confirmed identities receive individual records. Those without names are assigned numbers. Bodies too damaged to identify may be recorded by equipment, location and the testimony of survivors. Some are cremated together. Others are buried in numbered rows until a future identification becomes possible.
Its common saying is: “A man may be forgotten by the living. He must not be lost to the record.”
The Registry claims to preserve the identities of the dead. Its contradiction is that it increasingly reduces the living to entries awaiting closure.

E. Registry field manual — Identification of the Unreturned
    A body without a name is not an absence. It is an unresolved entry.
    Do not assign a name from resemblance alone. Do not close a record because a commander has declared the matter inconvenient. Do not use equipment as proof of identity when equipment has changed hands.
    Record the place of recovery, the condition of the body, the visible marks and the testimony of those present. If the body is incomplete, state that it is incomplete. If it is burned beyond recognition, state that it is burned beyond recognition.
    The dead are not improved by a more comforting description.
    A missing soldier remains missing until evidence establishes otherwise. A soldier presumed dead may still owe a debt, possess an inheritance or have a family awaiting confirmation.
    Do not remove identification plates before the record is made. Do not discard damaged tags. Do not combine names because the burial trench is full.
    If a body cannot be returned, return the record.
    If the record cannot be returned, make a second copy.
    If both are lost, the Registry has failed twice.
VIII. THE SALT COMPACT
“Nothing moves freely. Everything pays for passage.”
A. Institutional portrait
The Salt Compact descends from the customs authorities, transport administrators and trade-route operators that once coordinated the movement of goods between Architect settlements. Its predecessors controlled roads, bridges, river crossings, warehouses, ports and the systems used to record cargo.
The Silence did not end trade. It made every journey more dangerous and every shipment more valuable.
The Compact emerged from agreements between surviving transport authorities, customs officers and armed convoy operators. Their first purpose was to keep routes open. Their second was to ensure that the people using those routes paid for their protection. Their third was to prevent anyone else from controlling the same passage.
The Compact's authority is physical. It maintains chains across roads, toll gates at bridges, fortified warehouses and guarded transfer points. It controls the places where goods must stop. Its soldiers are trained to inspect, escort, seize and defend cargo. A convoy that refuses inspection may be treated as an enemy force.
Its records distinguish between cargo, transport, passage and loss. A wagon is not cargo. A horse is not a passenger. A broken wheel is not a lost shipment. The distinction matters because the Compact charges for each separately.
Its camps are built around wagons, crates, spare axles, portable chains and locked stores. Their soldiers wear practical armour designed for long journeys, wet weather and close fighting around vehicles. Hooks, short blades and chain fittings are common. Officers carry seals and cargo tallies rather than elaborate insignia.
They eat salted fish, hard bread, dried fruit, smoked meat and pickled vegetables. Fresh water is carefully rationed, especially on long routes. A convoy officer may drink better than the guards, but every ration is entered against the journey's costs.
The camps smell of salt, wet rope, tar, horse sweat, old fish and oxidised metal. Leather becomes stiff from salt exposure. Soldiers develop cracked lips, infected cuts and pale scars where rope burns have healed. Those who work around chains often lose skin from their palms and fingers.
The dead are stripped of useful clothing, weapons and transport equipment. Bodies lost along a route may be left until a return convoy can recover them. If a body is too expensive to retrieve, the Compact records the location and closes the transport account. Families may pay to reopen it.
Its common saying is: “The road remembers what the traveller owes.”
The Compact claims to keep the world connected. Its contradiction is that it controls movement so tightly that survival itself becomes a toll.

Towers are understood as temporary stopping places, not destinations. The creature's movement from tower to tower resembles a convoy that never reaches its final port. The Compact's officers sometimes describe the Dragons as the oldest stranded travellers in the world.
The arcane spire legend is treated as a possible route beyond the known sky. Some members believe the stars are navigational markers and that the spire would provide the final passage. Others consider this a romantic superstition that has no place in a transport ledger.
The Compact's official position is that no route has been verified.
E. Convoy regulations — Passage and Loss
    No cargo moves without a record. No record moves without a seal. No seal is valid after the route has been altered.
    The convoy master is responsible for the cargo. The escort captain is responsible for the road. The customs officer is responsible for the passage between them.
    Do not open a crate without two witnesses. Do not accept a seal that has been repaired. Do not allow a wagon to leave the column because its owner claims the contents are urgent.
    The road is not made safer by haste.
    If a wagon is lost, record its last verified position. If the driver is lost, record the cargo separately. If the cargo is recovered, the transport loss remains open until the wagon is accounted for.
    A dead guard does not cancel a toll. A dead merchant does not cancel a debt. A damaged bridge does not become free because the Compact must repair it.
    If the route is closed, post a notice at both ends. If the notice is removed, restore it. If the road cannot be held, deny passage to everyone.
    The road remembers what the traveller owes.

Potential collision to watch: The Iron Fists, Meridian Office, Gleaners' Communion, Sepulchral Registry and Salt Compact all share the same Monk visual foundation: black jacket, chainmail and iron gauntlets. Their faction distinction therefore depends on the shoulder cloth, small equipment and army-wide motif. Do not redesign the Monk's base uniform to solve this; use the faction identifiers already established.
PART IV — CONFLICTS MATRIX
The following twelve pairs are suggested natural conflicts for the map and team-game structure. They are not declarations of permanent alliances or fixed historical wars. Each conflict can produce rival branches, local campaigns or temporary coalitions.
Pair
Conflict
I. Iron Fists — II. Last Nave
Both claim authority over Spirit-bearing grounds, but the Iron Fists regard disciplined bodily exertion as the source of their practice while the Chapter insists that Spirit must be gathered and administered through proper rites.
I. Iron Fists — IV. Cinder Compact
The Compact wants the Order's gauntlet techniques standardised and reproduced through its foundries, while the Iron Fists refuse to surrender the craft secrets that define their military identity.
II. Last Nave — VII. Sepulchral Registry
The Chapter insists that the dead require rites, names and religious custody, while the Registry claims the legal authority to identify, classify and close their records.
II. Last Nave — VI. Gleaners' Communion
The Chapter demands that certain harvests and settlements remain under sacred protection, while the Communion treats food reserves as institutional property subject to allocation.
III. Verdant Warrant — VI. Gleaners' Communion
The Warrant restricts access to forests and timber according to its own surveys, while the Communion needs land cleared, fuel harvested and agricultural boundaries expanded.
III. Verdant Warrant — IV. Cinder Compact
The Compact's foundries consume timber and charcoal at a scale the Warrant considers destructive to controlled woodland reserves, while the Compact considers restrictions an attack on its industrial survival.
III. Verdant Warrant — V. Meridian Office
Both claim expertise over land measurement, but the Warrant treats forests as controlled production zones while the Office asserts that territorial boundaries and official maps determine jurisdiction.
IV. Cinder Compact — VIII. Salt Compact
The Cinder Compact requires uninterrupted movement of ore, fuel and finished weapons, while the Salt Compact controls the roads, crossings and toll points through which those materials must pass.
V. Meridian Office — VIII. Salt Compact
The Office claims the authority to define official routes and borders, while the Salt Compact controls the physical passage points and can make an officially recognised road unusable.
V. Meridian Office — VI. Gleaners' Communion
The Office's surveys can redraw settlement boundaries and reclassify agricultural land, threatening the Communion's inherited control over fields, granaries and local labour.
VII. Sepulchral Registry — IV. Cinder Compact
The Registry claims custody over the identities and remains of the dead, while the Compact considers unidentified corpses a source of recoverable material and often burns them after stripping equipment.
VII. Sepulchral Registry — VIII. Salt Compact
The Registry demands that bodies and casualty records be returned to their proper authorities, while the Compact refuses to spend transport resources recovering remains when the route contract does not cover them.
Map-design implications
These conflicts create several useful geographical pressures:
    Spirit-bearing territory: Iron Fists and Last Nave have a direct reason to contest it.
    Forests and timber routes: Verdant Warrant, Gleaners' Communion and Cinder Compact can compete over the same resource from different institutional positions.
    Roads, bridges and border crossings: Meridian Office and Salt Compact have overlapping but incompatible claims.
    Industrial supply routes: Cinder Compact and Salt Compact can fight without either needing to occupy the other's core territory.
    Burial grounds and battlefields: Sepulchral Registry can come into conflict with almost any power, especially those that reuse bodies, equipment or burial sites.
    Granaries and protected settlements: Gleaners' Communion and Last Nave can dispute whether food stores are sacred obligations or institutional reserves.
These are useful because they do not require every conflict to be a war of conquest. Some are disputes over who has the right to record, move, protect, consume or bury something.
PART V — TWO CONTRADICTORY DRAGON TEXTS
These texts represent competing institutional interpretations. Neither is an authoritative account of what Dragons are, what happened to them or whether they will return.
1. The Chapter of the Last Nave — The Witness at the Seventh Tower
The following is attributed to a Chapter archivist who accompanied a military procession to a tower in the northern ashlands.
    The creature arrived before dawn.
    We heard the stone complain first. Not a roar. Not a cry. A low grinding noise, as if the tower itself had remembered a weight it was never designed to carry.
    Then the wings opened.
    I have seen the painted saints in the old nave, their faces lifted towards the ceiling, their robes untouched by the dust of centuries. I thought the Dragon would resemble them. I was wrong. Its hide was split in places. Its wings shook when it tried to fold them. There was no radiance, no divine fire, no voice from above.
    Only exhaustion.
    Brother Halven called it a beast. I struck him for it.
    It had not come to hunt us. It had come to rest.
    We placed the crimson banner beneath its shadow and opened the book. The first prayer was interrupted by the creature lowering its head. Its eyes passed over the banner without recognition.
    I do not believe it understood our words. I do not believe it recognised our symbols. But when the bells began, it raised its head towards the sky.
    It remembered.
    I am certain of that much.
    The creature is not a beast that has learned to fly. It is something sacred that has been made to endure a world too low for it.
    The old prophecy speaks of the stars and the spire. We must not mistake its silence for refusal. It has been waiting longer than our books have existed.
    Heaven has not forgotten its own.
2. The Meridian Office — Report on an Unclassified Celestial Subject
The following is an extract from a restricted survey report. The author was later reprimanded for refusing to remove the final paragraph.
    Subject arrived at the tower at approximately the third watch.
    Estimated wingspan: inconsistent across observations.
    Landing weight: sufficient to damage the upper platform. Structural damage concentrated beneath the left side of the body. Subject displayed no visible interest in the survey party.
    Subject rested for an extended interval. Repeated measurements of its breathing, posture and movement indicate severe and persistent fatigue. The condition is not consistent with ordinary exertion.
    At sunrise, the subject raised its head and remained motionless for eleven minutes. Several witnesses interpreted this as a response to the stars. This conclusion is not supported by the observation record. The stars were no longer visible.
    Subject departed towards the east. Flight path was irregular. It selected a tower at a lower elevation than the previous one, despite the presence of higher structures within the recorded distance.
    Subject refused to land on the Arcane Tower.
    The Chapter claims that the creature is waiting to return to Heaven. The claim cannot be verified. More importantly, it assumes that the creature wishes to return.
    We have no evidence that the tower is a sanctuary. We have no evidence that the sky is its home. We have no evidence that its exhaustion is punishment.
    We have only a creature that rests, flies and rests again.
    The Chapter sees a sacred exile.
    I see a subject whose behaviour we have not yet understood.
    Neither interpretation belongs in a measurement record.

PART VII — FIVE QUESTIONS FOR LORE v4
These questions are intended to resolve structural issues rather than add decorative details.
1. How direct is the connection between the Architects' towers and the Dragon Exile?
    A. The Architects accidentally pierced or disrupted a boundary between the world and the Dragons' Sacred Place.
    B. The Architects deliberately constructed towers to reach or exploit something beyond the known sky.
    C. The connection is demonstrable, but the precise mechanism has been lost.
    D. Even the connection is disputed; only the chronology is known.
My recommendation remains C. It gives the history a real causal foundation without making the mystery too easy to solve.
2. What should a Dragon look like when its original celestial nature is considered?
    A. Almost divine: elegant, symmetrical and luminous, with exhaustion as the only visible corruption.
    B. Fallen and deteriorated: cracked scales, damaged wings, exposed anatomy and visible decay.
    C. Majestic anatomy corrupted by centuries of exhaustion: its original beauty is still recognisable, but the body has been worn down by an impossible separation.
My recommendation is C. It preserves the contrast between what the Dragons were and what they have become.
3. Should Spirit gathering physically alter Monks and Priests in different ways?
    A. Yes. Monks suffer physical deformation, muscular damage and changes to the body; Priests suffer neurological and sensory damage from formal rites.
    B. No. Both suffer the same broad bodily cost, but their methods and visible symptoms differ.
    C. Spirit gathering has no permanent visible effects; the cost is primarily internal.
My recommendation is A. It reinforces the distinction between bodily discipline and formal ritual.
4. How much Architect civilisation physically survives?
    A. Mostly dismantled ruins. Few functioning structures remain.
    B. Many abandoned mechanisms still operate unattended, sometimes for reasons nobody understands.
    C. Underground and enclosed complexes survive, while the surface world is built from the remains of the old civilisation.
My recommendation is a combination of B and C. The surface should be visibly dismantled, but there should be surviving complexes and unattended mechanisms that make the old civilisation feel genuinely enormous.
5. Did the eight institutions once cooperate, or did they arise independently?
    A. They once cooperated under a shared administrative structure and fractured after the Silence.
    B. They emerged independently from different surviving Architect sectors.
    C. They had an uneven relationship: some cooperated, some competed and some were originally subordinate to others.
My recommendation is C. It gives the institutions a shared history without making them feel like eight departments that simply changed their uniforms.
