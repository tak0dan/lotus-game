IDEAS v2 — The Tower Game
Version scope: Rules-grounded exploits, multiplayer elimination and yielding, and physical components.
The central correction for v2 is this:
A deliberate rule gap must be derivable from the implemented rules, not from a mechanic that sounds plausible.
I will use the supplied digest as the sole mechanical authority. I will not silently introduce new rules to make an exploit work.
There is also an important distinction throughout this document:
    Deliberate gap: The interaction is legal under the written rules, produces an interesting strategic possibility, and can be exploited through careful reading.
    Bug to fix: The interaction has no deterministic resolution, contradicts another rule, or can produce an unintended result that undermines the game.
    Potential gap: The interaction is real, but its outcome depends on a question the digest does not answer. It needs a wording decision before being accepted.
Coordinates below use (x,y), with north decreasing y and east increasing x.
TASK 1 — RULE GAPS
1. The Twelve Reworked Gaps
1. The Shared Tower Height Exploit
Keep as a deliberate gap
Source rules:
    Max 5 levels (+1 on highland, + the shared levels of 3 orthogonally attached own towers).
    Levels (one per tower per year, not on a tower founded this year; needs a friendly garrison or an adjacent friendly builder).
The exploit:
The maximum height of a tower depends on its neighbouring structures.
Imagine three orthogonally connected towers:
    Tower A: 4 levels.
    Tower B: 4 levels.
    Tower C: 4 levels.
A player can construct an additional level on one tower, provided all normal construction conditions are met.
The interesting interaction is that the three towers' shared levels contribute to their maximum height.
Resolution trace:
    Economy: Pay upkeep and resolve any disbandment.
    Recruitment: No relevant interaction.
    Movement: Position builders and garrisons.
    Combat: Defend the three towers.
    Building: Add a level to Tower A.
    Control: Resolve ownership.
    End of year: Calculate next year's economy.
The player can potentially develop a tower beyond the ordinary five-level maximum through the shared-level provision.
Why a careful reader finds it:
A skimmer sees the five-level limit and assumes it is absolute.
A careful reader notices the parenthetical exception.
Counter:
Attack the supporting towers. The attacker should investigate whether destroying a supporting structure changes the maximum height of its neighbours.
Important unresolved detail: The digest does not specify whether shared levels are calculated dynamically, whether all three towers must be connected to one another, or whether the relevant group must consist of exactly three towers. Those details must be established before this becomes a fully executable exploit.
2. Arcane Conversion After the Upkeep Calculation
Keep as a deliberate gap
Source rules:
    each year's cards = what nodes yield (computed at the end of the previous year), minus upkeep, which is paid first from Gold cards then Physical cards.
    Primary cards (Physical/Mana/Spirit) stand in for either of their two subtypes.
    Arcane conversion orders first (2 Soul + 2 Aether + 2 Ember into a finished non-fortress tower with no recruit in progress; a Dragon garrison blocks it)
The exploit:
A player can spend resources on Arcane conversion during Building, but that conversion cannot change the upkeep calculation that already occurred.
This creates a useful distinction between resources available for the current year and the resources generated by the resulting tower.
Resolution trace:
    End of previous year: Nodes determine the next year's income.
    Economy: Current budget is established and upkeep is deducted.
    Recruitment: Recruitment orders are processed.
    Movement: Units move.
    Combat: Combat resolves.
    Building: The player converts 2 Soul, 2 Aether and 2 Ember into a tower.
    Control: Strategic points are evaluated.
    End of year: The new tower may influence the next year's node yield, depending on its location and the relevant gathering rules.
The exploit is not escaping upkeep. It is investing resources after the upkeep obligation has already been settled.
Why a careful reader finds it:
A skimmer may treat all resource operations as part of one economic calculation.
A careful reader notices that conversion happens in Building, whereas upkeep is paid before Economy.
Counter:
Attack the player's resource-gathering units and deny access to the nodes that fund future conversions.
3. The Dragon's Forced Recruitment Perch
Keep as a deliberate gap
Source rules:
    units whose build time is up appear on a free orthogonal neighbour of their tower (if none is free they wait, unit stays at 0 years); a Dragon instead must perch at once on a free own tower of height 3+ (not Arcane), nearest first; if none, it dies.
    Dragon flights first (a Dragon may land on any other tower of height 3+, not Arcane, with no friendly or Dragon garrison; an enemy garrison is crushed and the tower changes owner).
The exploit:
Dragon recruitment and Dragon movement use different placement conditions.
Recruitment requires a free own tower.
Movement permits landing on another qualifying tower, including an enemy-occupied one, crushing its garrison.
A player can therefore exploit the distinction between the Dragon's initial placement and its subsequent flight.
Resolution trace:
    Recruitment: A Dragon's build time expires.
    Recruitment: It must perch on a qualifying free own tower. If none exists, it dies.
    Movement: The Dragon receives a flight order.
    Movement: It lands on another qualifying tower.
    If that tower has an enemy garrison, the garrison is crushed and ownership changes.
    Combat: Ordinary strike orders resolve.
    Building and Control: Remaining orders resolve normally.
Why a careful reader finds it:
A skimmer assumes Dragon recruitment and Dragon flight are variations of the same placement mechanic.
They are not.
Counter:
Do not leave qualifying towers undefended. A Dragon can convert an apparently secure enemy-held tower into a liability.
4. The Priest's Moving Protection
Keep as a deliberate gap
Source rules:
    A PRIEST lets each friendly unit within 2 squares (Chebyshev, not the Priest itself, Priest not idle) void one hit per year.
    If upkeep cannot be paid the most expensive units go IDLE (ignore orders and recruiting is blocked).
The exploit:
The Priest's protection is based on its position and status when the protection is evaluated, not on a permanently assigned ward.
The important combination is movement, idle status, and the Chebyshev radius.
Resolution trace:
    Economy: Upkeep is paid.
    Economy: If the Priest becomes idle, it ignores orders.
    Movement: An active Priest follows its submitted movement order.
    Combat: The Priest's position determines which friendly units are within two squares.
    Each qualifying friendly unit voids one hit for the year.
    The Priest itself receives no protection from its own ability.
Why a careful reader finds it:
A skimmer thinks of the Priest as providing a static protective zone.
A careful reader notices that the actual wording refers to units within two squares, and that the Priest must not be idle.
Counter:
Attack from multiple directions, separate the Priest from the units it protects, or make the Priest idle through economic pressure.
Potential ambiguity: Whether the Priest's position is checked once for the entire combat phase or separately for individual attacks needs explicit wording. The natural reading is one protection per unit per year, but the precise timing of the check matters.
5. The Monk's Exact-Return Trap
Bug to fix
Source rules:
    THEN each Monk that struck withdraws EXACTLY as many squares as it advanced (not more, not less) if such a square exists.
    a unit stops if the next square is occupied.
The exploit:
A Monk advances, strikes, and must withdraw exactly the number of squares it advanced.
However, its original route may no longer be available.
Resolution trace:
    Movement: Monk begins at (10,10).
    Movement: It advances three squares to (13,10).
    Combat: It strikes an adjacent enemy.
    Combat: Another combat interaction or simultaneous movement has left (12,10) occupied.
    Combat: The Monk attempts to withdraw three squares.
    The exact-return condition conflicts with the occupied square.
The digest does not establish whether the Monk:
    Remains in place.
    Withdraws partially.
    Dies.
    Cannot make the strike in the first place.
Why a careful reader finds it:
The exact-return requirement is unusually strict. A skimmer assumes the Monk simply goes back.
A careful reader realizes that the original route may no longer exist.
Counter:
Occupy the Monk's withdrawal route before the withdrawal occurs.
My recommendation: Preserve the exact-return requirement, but explicitly define what happens when the route becomes unavailable. Otherwise, this is not a useful loophole; it is an unresolved state.
6. Heavy Void and the Monk's Gauntlets
Keep as a deliberate gap
Source rules:
    A HEAVY unit voids one hit per year, but only a hit from its light version, a ranged unit or a Dragon (unless the striker is a Monk with Runic Gauntlets).
The exploit:
Heavy units have a defensive exception specifically tied to the Monk's equipment.
A Monk with Runic Gauntlets bypasses the Heavy unit's void.
Resolution trace:
    Movement: The Monk reaches an orthogonally adjacent Heavy unit.
    Combat: The Monk submits a strike order.
    Combat: The strike is validated against post-movement positions.
    Combat: The strike deals one hit.
    The Heavy unit's void does not apply because the striker is a Monk with Runic Gauntlets.
    The hit contributes to the Heavy unit's yearly damage requirement.
Why a careful reader finds it:
A skimmer reads Heavy as a generally durable unit.
A careful reader notices the exception buried at the end of the rule.
Counter:
Prevent the Monk from reaching melee range, use Priest protection, or eliminate the Monk before its strike resolves.
7. Primary Cards and Subtype Substitution
Keep as a deliberate gap
Source rules:
    Primary cards (Physical/Mana/Spirit) stand in for either of their two subtypes.
    Unspent cards vanish at year end.
The exploit:
A primary card can substitute for either subtype of its resource family.
The player can therefore preserve flexibility until the order is committed.
Resolution trace:
    Economy: Calculate the yearly budget.
    Economy: Pay upkeep, first Gold and then Physical.
    Recruitment: Spend cards on recruit orders.
    Movement: Submit and resolve movement.
    Combat: Submit and resolve attacks.
    Building: Use primary cards as their permitted subtypes when paying for the relevant orders.
    End of year: Any remaining cards vanish.
The strategic advantage is not card accumulation. It is flexible allocation within the current year.
Why a careful reader finds it:
A skimmer treats primary cards as a fixed resource type.
A careful reader sees that the card represents a choice between two subtypes.
Counter:
Force the player to spend their flexible resources on competing priorities. Do not assume they have committed to one subtype until their orders reveal it.
Important unresolved detail: The digest does not specify whether the subtype is chosen when the order is written or when it resolves. That timing should be fixed.
8. The Deliberate Upkeep Collapse
Keep as a deliberate gap
Source rules:
    If upkeep cannot be paid the most expensive units go IDLE (ignore orders and recruiting is blocked).
    Unspent cards vanish at year end.
The exploit:
A player can recruit an expensive army, knowing that future upkeep may exceed their available budget.
This creates a temporary military advantage in exchange for future inactivity.
Resolution trace:
    Previous year: Player recruits several expensive units.
    End of year: Node yields determine the next year's income.
    Next Economy: Upkeep is calculated and paid.
    If insufficient, the most expensive units become idle.
    Idle units ignore orders.
    Recruiting is blocked.
    The player must survive with a partially inactive army.
Why a careful reader finds it:
A skimmer treats upkeep as a constraint that prevents over-recruitment.
A careful reader realizes that the rule does not prohibit recruitment that creates future unaffordability.
Counter:
Attack the player's income sources and force the economic collapse before they can use the expensive army effectively.
9. Disbandment Before Recruitment
Keep as a deliberate gap
Source rules:
    ECONOMY phase: disband orders execute (unit removed, no battle loss); research is paid and completes.
    RECRUITMENT phase: units whose build time is up appear on a free orthogonal neighbour of their tower.
The exploit:
A unit can be deliberately disbanded during Economy, freeing a square before Recruitment checks for available placement.
Resolution trace:
Assume a tower at (20,20) has all four orthogonal neighbouring squares occupied.
A unit is standing at (20,19).
    Economy: The player issues a disband order for the unit at (20,19).
    Economy: The unit is removed without battle loss.
    Recruitment: A completed unit checks the tower's orthogonal neighbours.
    Recruitment: (20,19) is now free.
    Recruitment: The new unit appears there.
The disbanded unit effectively creates a recruitment slot.
Why a careful reader finds it:
A skimmer thinks of disbandment as an economic decision.
A careful reader notices that it happens before recruitment placement.
Counter:
Occupy the remaining available recruitment squares and force the opponent to choose between keeping an expensive unit or creating space for a new one.
10. Terrain as a Movement Filter, Not a Universal Restriction
Keep as a deliberate gap
Source rules:
    Rock and deep water block everything; forest blocks Cavalry and Siege.
    ground moves advance one square per step in lockstep, orthogonal only.
The exploit:
Forest restrictions apply specifically to Cavalry and Siege.
They do not apply to every unit.
Resolution trace:
    Movement: Infantry enters a forest square.
    Movement: Cavalry attempts to enter the same forest square.
    The Cavalry cannot enter.
    Movement: A different unit type can still use the forest, provided it has movement remaining and the square is otherwise legal.
    Combat: The forest can therefore create asymmetric access to territory.
Why a careful reader finds it:
A skimmer sees terrain as a broad movement penalty.
A careful reader notices that the forest restriction names only two unit classes.
Counter:
Use Cavalry and Siege on alternative routes, and control the approaches to forest terrain with units that can enter it.
This is a relatively simple gap, but it is strategically meaningful because the terrain rules are unit-specific.
11. The Empty Enemy Tower Seizure
Potential gap — needs a tie-break rule
Source rules:
    a tower base can only be climbed as the last step (+1 MOV), an empty enemy tower is seized on entry.
    ground moves advance one square per step in lockstep, orthogonal only; a unit stops if the next square is occupied; enemies entering the same square both stop and CLASH (fight each other in combat).
The exploit:
The tower is empty, so the normal occupied-square collision does not prevent entry.
Two enemy units may attempt to enter the same empty enemy tower base during the same movement resolution.
Resolution trace:
    Player A's Infantry stands at (30,29).
    Player B's Infantry stands at (30,31).
    Both submit orders to enter the enemy tower base at (30,30).
    Movement: Both units advance simultaneously.
    Both attempt to enter the same square.
    The tower is initially empty.
    The digest does not explicitly define how simultaneous entry into the tower base interacts with empty-tower seizure.
Possible outcomes include a clash, a priority seizure, or a rejected movement.
Why a careful reader finds it:
A skimmer interprets the collision rule as covering all contested squares.
A careful reader notices that the tower seizure rule is separate and does not explicitly describe simultaneous entry.
Counter:
Defend the tower with a garrison or occupy the approach squares.
My recommendation: Fix the tie-break. Both units should clash if they enter the same square, and neither should seize the tower before the clash resolves. Otherwise, player resolution order could produce arbitrary ownership.
12. Healing at the End of the Year
Keep as a deliberate gap
Source rules:
    Every strike is exactly one hit. A unit dies when it has taken as many hits as it needs that year.
    Everything that survived heals.
The exploit:
A unit can survive multiple strikes during the year and then recover completely at the end of the year.
The yearly damage requirement creates a threshold, not a permanent accumulation of damage.
Resolution trace:
    Movement: A unit enters combat range.
    Combat: It receives one hit.
    Combat: It receives another hit.
    Combat: It survives because it has not reached its yearly hit requirement.
    Building and Control: The unit remains on the board.
    End of year: It heals fully.
Why a careful reader finds it:
A skimmer may assume damage persists between years.
A careful reader notices the explicit healing rule.
Counter:
Concentrate strikes during one year. Spreading attacks across multiple years can allow the target to recover between engagements.
This is an important part of the game's chess-like identity: combat is about reaching a threshold within a year, not gradually accumulating conventional hit points.
2. Five Additional Gaps (13–17)
These are additional interactions, not replacements for the twelve above. They concentrate on the less obvious consequences of phase ordering and simultaneous resolution.
13. The Simultaneous Empty-Tower Seizure
Bug to fix
Source rules:
    an empty enemy tower is seized on entry.
    ground moves advance one square per step in lockstep, orthogonal only; a unit stops if the next square is occupied; enemies entering the same square both stop and CLASH (fight each other in combat).
The exploit:
Two enemies can approach an empty tower from different directions and attempt to seize it simultaneously.
Resolution trace:
    Player A has Infantry at (40,39).
    Player B has Infantry at (40,41).
    The empty enemy tower occupies (40,40).
    Both submit movement orders into (40,40).
    Movement resolves in lockstep.
    Both units attempt to enter the same square.
    The collision rule says they CLASH.
    The tower seizure rule says an empty enemy tower is seized on entry.
The unresolved question is whether either player gets ownership before the clash.
Why a careful reader finds it:
The two rules are individually clear. Their interaction is not.
Counter:
Keep the tower garrisoned or control its approaches.
Verdict: Fix the resolution. The clash should occur before ownership is assigned. If both units die in combat, the tower should remain empty and retain its previous ownership.
This is preferable to giving priority to whichever player happens to resolve first.
14. The Recruitment Window That Closes Before Movement
Keep as a deliberate gap
Source rules:
    units whose build time is up appear on a free orthogonal neighbour of their tower (if none is free they wait, unit stays at 0 years).
    YEAR ORDER: economy, recruitment, movement, combat, building, control, end of year.
The exploit:
A completed recruit checks for a free square before movement happens.
A square that becomes free later that year cannot be used until the next recruitment phase.
Resolution trace:
A tower occupies (50,50).
Its four neighbouring squares are occupied:
    (50,49)
    (50,51)
    (49,50)
    (51,50)
    Economy: The player pays upkeep.
    Recruitment: A completed unit checks the four neighbouring squares.
    All four are occupied.
    The unit remains waiting at zero years.
    Movement: One friendly unit moves from (50,49) to (50,48).
    Combat: Resolves.
    Building: Resolves.
    End of year: The recruitment remains waiting.
    Next year: Recruitment checks the available squares again.
Why a careful reader finds it:
A skimmer thinks of recruitment and movement as simultaneous board-management activities.
A careful reader follows the actual phase order.
Counter:
Occupy recruitment squares before the opponent's recruitment phase, or force the opponent to maintain a permanent blocking unit.
Verdict: Keep. This is a particularly good strategic gap because it rewards planning one year ahead.
15. The Idle Disband Paradox
Potential gap — clarify the rule
Source rules:
    If upkeep cannot be paid the most expensive units go IDLE (ignore orders and recruiting is blocked).
    ECONOMY phase: disband orders execute (unit removed, no battle loss).
The exploit:
Disbandment happens in Economy, but an unaffordable unit becomes idle because of upkeep.
The question is whether the idle status prevents its disband order.
Resolution trace:
    The player has insufficient cards to pay all upkeep.
    The most expensive unit becomes idle.
    The player has also submitted a disband order for that unit.
    Economy begins and disbandment orders execute.
    The idle rule says the unit ignores orders.
    The disband rule says disband orders execute during Economy.
Two interpretations are possible:
    Idle units ignore disband orders, so the unit remains.
    Disbandment is an Economy operation that occurs before idle status is applied, so the unit disappears.
The digest does not explicitly establish the ordering of these two operations.
Why a careful reader finds it:
A skimmer sees the disband rule and assumes all submitted disband orders execute.
A careful reader notices that idle units ignore orders and questions whether this includes Economy orders.
Counter:
Avoid relying on disbandment to rescue an unaffordable unit until the timing is defined.
Verdict: Fix. I recommend that upkeep determines idle status first, followed by Economy disbandment, with idle units unable to disband themselves.
That makes economic failure consequential rather than conveniently reversible.
16. The Arcane Tower and the Queued Dragon
Bug to fix
Source rules:
    Arcane conversion orders first (2 Soul + 2 Aether + 2 Ember into a finished non-fortress tower with no recruit in progress; a Dragon garrison blocks it).
    units whose build time is up appear on a free orthogonal neighbour of their tower; a Dragon instead must perch at once on a free own tower of height 3+ (not Arcane), nearest first; if none, it dies.
The exploit:
A Dragon can be queued for recruitment while a player simultaneously submits an Arcane conversion order.
The Arcane conversion requires that there be no recruit in progress.
The digest does not define whether a Dragon whose build time expires during the same year counts as a recruit in progress when conversion is checked.
Resolution trace:
    Previous year: The player queues a Dragon at Tower A.
    New year, Economy: The Dragon's recruitment timer is complete.
    Recruitment: The Dragon attempts to perch on a qualifying own tower.
    The player also has an Arcane conversion order targeting Tower B.
    Combat resolves.
    Building: Arcane conversion orders resolve first.
    The conversion checks whether a recruit is in progress.
    If the completed Dragon is no longer considered in progress, Tower B can be converted.
    If it remains in progress until it perches, conversion may be rejected.
Why a careful reader finds it:
A skimmer thinks of recruitment completion as a simple transition.
A careful reader notices that Dragon placement happens in Recruitment, while Arcane conversion checks recruitment status later in Building.
Counter:
Use the Dragon's completed recruitment to force the opponent to consider the availability of qualifying perches before committing to Arcane construction.
Verdict: Fix the definition. Once recruitment is completed in the Recruitment phase, the Dragon is no longer a recruit in progress. Its placement outcome is already resolved.
An Arcane conversion should therefore be permitted if the other requirements are met.
17. The Rotating First Player Advantage
Potential gap — needs a defined scope
Source rules:
    All players plan secretly and simultaneously; the order in which players are resolved rotates each year (first player cycles).
    Dragon flights first.
    Siege orders then resolve in sequence.
The exploit:
Although orders are simultaneous, certain operations resolve sequentially.
This means the rotating first-player position could matter whenever two players submit competing orders that require a priority decision.
Resolution trace:
    Year 1: Player A is first.
    Player A and Player B submit simultaneous orders.
    Movement: Both attempt to interact with the same tower.
    If the interaction is resolved by player priority, Player A may benefit.
    Year 2: Player B becomes first.
    The same interaction may now favour Player B.
Siege orders are especially interesting because they explicitly resolve in sequence.
Suppose two players siege the same tower during the same year:
    Player A deals one structural damage.
    Player B deals one structural damage.
    The top level reaches zero.
    The level falls.
If there are more siege orders than structural damage needed, the remaining orders might interact with the newly exposed level.
Why a careful reader finds it:
A skimmer assumes simultaneous orders mean simultaneous outcomes.
A careful reader distinguishes between simultaneous submission and sequential resolution.
Counter:
Avoid depending on an opponent's first-player position. Build redundant siege capability and plan for both resolution orders.
Verdict: Keep the rotating order, but specify exactly which operations use player priority and which resolve simultaneously.
The rotating player order should never silently override an explicit simultaneous-resolution rule.

TASK 2 — MULTIPLAYER RULES
4. The Vacant Throne
Proposed rules text — Multiplayer Elimination v1.0
Purpose: Elimination must change the strategic situation without removing the eliminated player's physical presence from the board or forcing them to watch an empty seat for several hours.
4.1. Trigger
A player is eliminated when their fortress has been held by an enemy through a full year, as defined by the normal victory rules.
Elimination occurs at the relevant End of Year phase.
The eliminated player immediately loses control of their faction.
4.2. Towers and structures
Upon elimination:
    All towers belonging to the eliminated player remain on the board.
    All tower levels remain intact.
    All attached towers remain attached.
    All fortresses remain physically present.
    All structures become neutral.
    No player automatically inherits ownership.
A neutral tower cannot receive orders, recruit units, or construct new levels.
It can still be attacked and besieged according to normal rules.
4.3. Garrisoned units
All surviving units become Ruin Garrison units.
Their rules:
    They remain in their current squares.
    They cannot receive movement, strike, recruitment, or building orders.
    They cannot initiate combat.
    They continue occupying squares.
    They can be attacked and killed under normal combat rules.
    They receive no Priest protection unless a separate active Priest protects them.
    They require no upkeep.
They are therefore physical remnants of the fallen faction, not an active neutral army.
Important: A Ruin Garrison is not a new unit type. It is a status applied to surviving units.
4.4. Ungarrisoned units
Surviving units outside towers also become Ruin Garrison units.
They remain exactly where they are.
They are not automatically disbanded or relocated.
This can create interesting situations where a neutral remnant blocks a road, prevents recruitment, or temporarily occupies a strategic location.
4.5. Resource nodes
Resource nodes remain on the map.
Their ownership is not transferred to another player.
    Nodes previously controlled by the eliminated player become unclaimed.
    Normal gathering rules still apply.
    Active players can begin gathering from unclaimed nodes using the required units or structures.
    A neutral Arcane Tower on a node produces no usable resource for any player.
This prevents a defeated player from gifting their entire economic network to an ally through elimination.
4.6. Strategic points
Strategic points previously controlled by the eliminated player become unclaimed.
They no longer contribute to that player's score.
Another player must satisfy the normal control conditions to claim them.
4.7. Cards and research
The eliminated player loses:
    All current-year cards.
    All unspent orders.
    All research progress not already completed.
Completed research remains part of the game's historical state but has no active owner.
No cards are transferred to another player.
4.8. Victory conditions
The eliminated player no longer counts as an active side.
Their points are removed from the strategic-point calculation.
Their structures and neutral remnants do not count toward another player's victory unless the normal control rules are satisfied.
5. The Last Testament
Proposed rules text — Multiplayer Elimination v1.0
The Last Testament gives an eliminated player one final strategic decision.
It should be meaningful without allowing a defeated player to decide the entire game out of spite.
5.1. Eligibility
An eliminated player receives one Last Testament when eliminated through the normal fortress-elimination rule.
The order must be submitted secretly at the same time as the next year's orders.
5.2. Available testament actions
The player chooses exactly one:
Action
Effect
Final March
One surviving unit makes a normal movement order.
Final Strike
One surviving unit submits one normal strike order.
Final Siege
One surviving unit submits one normal siege order.
Final Dissolution
One surviving unit is removed through disbandment.
Restrictions:
    Only a unit that belonged to the eliminated player may be selected.
    The selected unit must still exist when the order resolves.
    No recruitment, research, conversion, or construction is permitted.
    The testament does not provide additional resource cards.
    Normal terrain, movement, combat, and siege restrictions still apply.
The selected unit executes its final order at the appropriate phase and becomes a Ruin Garrison immediately afterward if it survives.
5.3. Order resolution
The Last Testament is submitted before the next year begins.
It resolves in the ordinary phase corresponding to the selected action.
It does not receive priority over active players.
The Last Testament cannot be modified after submission.
If the selected unit is destroyed before its action resolves, the testament fails.
5.4. Why this works
The eliminated player can:
    Complete one final attack.
    Move a remnant away from an important location.
    Damage a tower.
    Remove a strategically obstructive unit.
But they cannot recruit an army, transfer an entire kingdom, or launch a second independent war.
The Last Testament gives agency without turning elimination into a kingmaking mechanism.
6. Team Games and the Vacant Throne
Team games need one important distinction:
A player is not necessarily the same thing as a side.
For this ruleset, a side is the unit that competes for victory. A side can consist of multiple players.
I recommend these rules:
Situation
Result
One team member loses their fortress
That player's seat is eliminated.
Other teammates remain active
The team continues playing.
Eliminated teammate's towers
Become neutral.
Eliminated teammate's garrisons
Become Ruin Garrisons.
Strategic points
Become unclaimed.
Remaining teammates
Receive no automatic inheritance.
Entire team loses its last active player
The side is eliminated.
A team member's elimination does not automatically eliminate their teammates.
Team Last Testament
The eliminated member may still submit their one Last Testament.
However:
    They cannot issue orders for their teammates.
    They cannot transfer units or structures to teammates.
    They cannot give their teammates additional budget cards.
    Their testament resolves under normal priority rules.
This is important because otherwise a team could use one member as a disposable strategic resource.
7. The Yield Action — Honourable Surrender
New core mechanic proposal
This is the most interesting addition from the Jihui influence.
A player should be able to acknowledge a balanced or hopeless position without being treated as a defeated player.
The difference is that yielding is a voluntary political decision, not an act of military elimination.
7.1. Yield declaration
A player may submit a YIELD order during the Economy phase.
The order must identify one active opposing player.
It is secret until the beginning of the year's resolution.
The declaration cannot be withdrawn after the orders are revealed.
7.2. What happens when a player yields?
The player:
    Immediately ends their participation.
    Receives the status Honourably Yielded.
    Loses control of their towers and structures.
    Converts their surviving units into Ruin Garrisons.
    Releases their resource nodes and strategic points.
    Loses their remaining cards and orders.
    Receives no Last Testament.
The named recipient does not automatically inherit the yielded player's territory.
That territory becomes neutral, exactly as under the Vacant Throne rules.
This is essential.
If yielding automatically transferred the entire faction to the named player, it would become a kingmaking tool.
7.3. The Honour reward
The named recipient receives one public Honour Mark.
Honour Marks are not resource cards and cannot pay upkeep or orders.
They have two purposes:
First: Recognition
Honour Marks are recorded separately from strategic points.
They represent the number of players who voluntarily acknowledged the recipient's position.
Second: Tie-breaking
If multiple players satisfy the same victory condition in the same End of Year resolution, the player with more Honour Marks wins the tie.
In team games, Honour Marks are recorded for the named player but count toward their team's tie-break total.
7.4. The cost of honour
Yielding carries a meaningful cost:
    The yielding player immediately forfeits their own victory opportunity.
    They cannot issue a Last Testament.
    They cannot name a teammate as recipient.
    They cannot transfer their territory or units.
    Their faction becomes neutral, not an extension of the recipient.
This means the act of yielding recognizes another player's position without handing them a kingdom.
7.5. Why yield instead of fighting?
There are three strategic reasons:
    Avoiding pointless elimination: A player in an unwinnable position can end their participation voluntarily.
    Political recognition: A player can publicly acknowledge an opponent's superiority.
    Standstill recognition: A player can yield when they believe another player has achieved a strategically decisive position, even if the formal victory condition has not yet triggered.
7.6. The Jihui principle
I would add one more rule:
A player may not yield while their side holds more than half of all strategic points.
This prevents a player who is already winning from using yielding to manufacture additional Honour Marks.
However, there is an interesting alternative:
Allow a player to yield even while winning, but make Honour Marks irrelevant to the primary victory conditions.
That would make yielding purely ceremonial and political.
My preference is the first approach. It makes Honour a scarce recognition of genuine strategic superiority.
7.7. The standstill scenario
For a special Jihui-inspired scenario, introduce a different victory condition:
    If no player can achieve a victory condition for three consecutive years, the game enters a Standstill.
    Players may negotiate a settlement.
    A player may yield to another.
    If all remaining players yield or agree to a draw, the game ends in a Balanced Peace.
The winner is not necessarily the player who conquered the most territory.
The winner may be the player who earned the recognition of the others.
This should be a scenario-specific victory condition, not part of ordinary competitive play.

Five Questions for IDEAS v3
These questions are deliberately more technical. Their answers will determine whether the next version can turn the proposed mechanics into precise rules text.
1. How exactly is shared tower height calculated?
When three orthogonally attached towers share levels, is the additional maximum height based on the sum of all three towers' levels, and does the relationship need to be reciprocal? Can multiple groups of three overlap?
This is particularly important because it determines whether a carefully constructed network can produce towers substantially taller than five levels.
2. What happens when multiple units attempt to enter the same tower base simultaneously?
Should the normal CLASH rule apply before tower ownership changes, or should tower seizure take precedence?
I recommend CLASH first, ownership second.
3. Does IDLE status apply to every order, including disbandment and research-related operations?
The current digest says idle units ignore orders, but it does not explicitly define the interaction with Economy-phase operations.
I recommend making IDLE a unit-wide status that prevents all orders issued by that unit.
4. Can a player voluntarily yield to an opponent who is already on their own team?
I recommend prohibiting this in ordinary team games. Otherwise, teammates could manufacture Honour Marks through coordinated surrender.
5. Should Honour be a competitive resource, a political reputation system, or an entirely separate victory condition?
My current proposal uses Honour only as a tie-breaker. However, the Jihui influence might justify an entire scenario built around honourable yielding, mutual recognition, and balanced standstill.
The most important design decision before v3: Establish the exact semantics of simultaneous resolution. The game already has enough interacting systems to produce complex strategies. It does not need additional mechanics to become interesting; it needs precise rules describing what happens when several individually legal actions collide.
