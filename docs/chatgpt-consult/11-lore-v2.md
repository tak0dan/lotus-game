AURELIA
Scope: Historical canon, four fully developed institutions, four provisional institutions, miniature production specifications and in-world texts.
Production standard: This document is written for physical miniatures at approximately 28 mm scale. Colours, silhouettes, materials and identifying details are intended to remain distinguishable after painting, weathering and tabletop use.
0. Foundational canon
Several points from LORE v1 are retained. Others are explicitly replaced.
The world
Aurelia is a continent that survived its civilisation but lost the systems that made that civilisation function.
The Architects of Silence were ordinary people. They were not gods, supernatural beings or a superior species. Their extraordinary achievement was the construction of systems so extensive that generations eventually stopped understanding the difference between maintaining civilisation and maintaining the machinery of civilisation.
They treated civilisation as merely another variable.
They measured population, production, territory, magical output, mortality and military expenditure. Eventually, they began treating the things being measured as interchangeable with the measurements themselves.
The infrastructure survived them.
The people inherited it.
The Silence Reckoning
    BS — Before Silence.
    AS — After Silence.
    0 AS — Collapse of the central Architect administrative network.
The historical sequence from LORE v1 remains provisionally established, but the Dragon explanation is replaced completely. The Exile is no longer a natural-species migration or an environmental consequence.
The Dragons

Dragons are celestial beings. They came from a place beyond Aurelia, which surviving traditions variously call Heaven, the Sacred Place or the Upper Kingdom.
Nobody alive knows why they were exiled. Nobody possesses reliable evidence of the circumstances, the authority responsible or the conditions of their possible return.
Their exhaustion is permanent. They are not ordinary animals that become tired after flying. They are beings deprived of the place and conditions in which their original nature could exist.
They rest only on towers.
A tower is the nearest thing to the heavens they can reach. Greater height provides their only respite. They move between elevated structures because remaining on the ground means enduring an existence for which they were never made.
The Architects' construction of towers is causally connected to the Dragons' exile, but the exact relationship remains unresolved.
The working historical hypothesis is that the Architects' earliest monumental towers interfered with a celestial boundary or structure. Their construction coincided with the first documented manifestations of the Exile. Whether they caused it, facilitated it or merely made it observable is unknown.
There is an old belief:
When the stars align and the arcane spire pierces the sky, the Dragons will return.
An Arcane Tower repels Dragons. Its particular composition is incompatible with their presence.
This is an established game-world property, not proof of the legend.
The Arcane Tower does not necessarily bring the Dragons closer to their home. It may simply be the one structure they cannot endure.
The Dragon's tragedy: Its body remembers a place it can no longer reach.
Magic
Magic is measurable, geometric and derived from Architect machinery.
It is never clean.
Aether and Ember are extracted through mechanisms, instruments and controlled processes. Spirit is gathered through living bodies and ritual practices: Monks gather it instinctively through their discipline, while Priests gather it through formal rites.
Magic consumes something.
Aether mechanisms leave burns, tremors and sensory damage. Ember processes leave blackened tissue, scarred lungs and persistent heat beneath the skin. Spirit rites leave their practitioners physically diminished.
The Architect instruments measure output. They do not measure suffering particularly well.
The institutions
The eight powers are institutions, not nations.
They can divide, reform, schism and fight their own branches. Two armies bearing the same banner may consider each other heretics, usurpers or administrative frauds.
Their identities are visual and narrative at this stage. No faction-specific mechanical bonuses or exceptions are introduced.

A. Institutional portrait
The Iron Fists began as a collection of monastic schools that preserved physical disciplines after the collapse of the Architects. Their original purpose was not military. They taught movement, endurance, pain tolerance, breath control and the preservation of bodily techniques that could not be recorded reliably in books.
The Fall changed their curriculum.
Their monasteries were built near Spirit-bearing grounds, and their practitioners discovered that disciplined physical exertion could gather what their records called Spirit. The first military conflicts over these grounds transformed the schools into armed institutions. Their students became guards. Their instructors became commanders. Their training yards became recruitment grounds.
The Order of Heavy Metal is the most influential surviving branch, although the names Iron Fists and Fists of the Renegade remain in use.
A Monk does not wear knightly armour. Chainmail lies beneath a leather school jacket, usually repaired so many times that the original stitching has disappeared. The gauntlets are different. They are thick, plated instruments of violence, often weighing enough to deform an untrained person's wrists.
The Order's greatest humiliation is its treatment of injury. A broken hand is not an excuse to abandon training. The damaged fingers are wrapped, splinted and forced to retain their positions during recovery. Knuckles frequently heal crooked. Some older Monks cannot fully close their hands without first warming them over a brazier.
Their monasteries smell of wet leather, old blood, iron filings and boiled medicinal roots.
Recruitment begins young, but survival is not guaranteed. Novices are made to carry stones, strike padded iron posts and maintain positions until their muscles fail. Those who cannot continue are assigned to maintenance, cooking or burial duties.
The Order's cruelty is institutional rather than theatrical. It does not punish pain. It punishes the inability to function despite pain.
Runic Gauntlets are a later development. Fire-runes are carved into the gauntlets and activated during impact. The resulting heat and force are directed into the opponent's armour, where they cause catastrophic internal burns. The Order considers this a refinement of discipline, not a departure from it.
Their dead are stripped of usable equipment. Gauntlets are returned to the school. The bodies are buried beneath the training yards, with the hands left uncovered until the final rite.
Their doctrine remains unchanged:
The body is truth. The path is law. Discipline is faith.

A. Institutional portrait
The Chapter of the Last Nave descends from the custodians of the great cathedrals. Before the Silence, these buildings were simultaneously places of worship, administrative centres, archives and magical infrastructure. Their custodians maintained records, controlled access and supervised rites that connected local populations to the wider Architect system.
After the Fall, they inherited buildings they could no longer maintain.
The Dismantling was not a single act of sacrilege. It was a prolonged administrative decision. Iron was needed. The cathedral walls were thick. Their frameworks contained precisely the materials required for fortifications and military machinery.
The Chapter's ancestors resisted until resistance became more expensive than surrender. They began removing their own buildings piece by piece, retaining the most recognisable architectural elements as sacred relics.
The War Priest emerged from this practice.
A cathedral is not worn as armour for appearance. Its structural components provide protection, while its spires and arches reproduce the familiar geometry of the old sanctuaries. The crown behind the Priest's head is an architectural frame rather than a conventional helmet ornament. The open prayer book contains rites, names and instructions for gathering Spirit.
The banner is not optional. It is a portable statement that the Chapter's authority remains present even when its buildings are gone.
A Priest's wards protect nearby soldiers. The protection has a physical price. Each warding rite requires the Priest to absorb part of the spiritual burden it prevents from reaching the soldiers. After prolonged engagements, their hands become stiff and discoloured. Fingernails split. Small vessels rupture beneath the skin. The palms often bear geometric scars matching the patterns in the prayer book.
The Chapter's hospitals are cold, damp rooms beneath the remaining sanctuaries. They smell of candle grease, wet stone, incense, infected wounds and boiled linen.
Its recruitment is built around obligation. A family that receives protection from the Chapter may owe a child, a portion of its harvest or years of labour.
The dead are separated into three categories: those whose names are recorded, those whose equipment can be recovered and those whose identities have been lost. The third category is especially feared. An unnamed dead person receives no individual burial rite.
The Chapter does not consider itself cruel. It considers cruelty a necessary expenditure when maintaining the sacred order.
Its greatest tragedy is that it continues preserving the forms of a civilisation whose original beliefs it no longer understands.

III. THE VERDANT WARRANT
INSTITUTION III — MILITARY FORESTRY AND MARKSMAN CORPS

A. Institutional portrait
The Verdant Warrant descends from the military forestry and hunting authorities of the Architects. Their original responsibility was to manage controlled woodland, maintain protected hunting grounds, monitor animal populations and secure remote infrastructure. They were not guardians of nature. They were responsible for making the land produce predictable results.
Their records contain measurements of tree density, soil conditions, animal movement, timber yield and the maximum sustainable extraction rate of entire forests. The Warrant inherited these records and the personnel trained to interpret them.
After the Silence, the institution discovered that its knowledge was more useful for killing people than managing trees.
The Warrant does not romanticise woodland warfare. Its soldiers are not wandering hunters, and its Archers are not picturesque bowmen. They are trained to kill armoured soldiers at distances where ordinary infantry cannot retaliate effectively.
Their heavy bows are substantial military instruments. Reinforced limbs, thick strings and carefully manufactured arrows allow them to punch through ordinary infantry armour. Drawing one requires considerable strength and training. The Warrant's armourers inspect bow limbs after every engagement and reject even minor structural defects.
Their powder-blue armour is deliberately distinctive. Copper fittings and scratches cover its surfaces, while camouflage capes combine forest green, powder blue and brown. The cape disrupts the outline of the wearer against mixed terrain. It does not make the soldier invisible. It makes identifying the precise location of a firing position difficult.
The Warrant is frightening because its soldiers do not shout before firing. They measure. They communicate using small hand movements, short whistles and marked distances.
Its recruitment system is particularly unpleasant. Children from controlled settlements are tested for eyesight, hand stability and draw strength. Those who fail are assigned to forestry labour. Those who pass are entered into a military register, often before their families receive the final harvest allocation.
Their camps smell of wet canvas, copper polish, bowstring wax, animal fat and the sharp chemical odour of arrow treatment.
Dead soldiers are searched for usable arrows, equipment and identification plates. Bodies are left until the firing positions have been secured. In woodland operations, the dead may be buried beneath the very trees their institution once measured.
The Warrant's doctrine is simple: a soldier who can be seen can be killed. A soldier who can be measured can be eliminated.

IV. THE CINDER COMPACT
INSTITUTION IV — INDUSTRIAL FOUNDRY CONSORTIUM
CHARACTER Art by Immersturm - Free | Tensor.Art | Tensor.Art
A. Institutional portrait
The Cinder Compact formed from the foundries and smelting settlements that survived the Dismantling. Its original members were independent ironmasters, furnace operators, casting supervisors and owners of industrial workshops. They had little interest in territorial government. They wanted access to ore, fuel, labour and transport.
The first Compact was an agreement about production. Its signatories promised to maintain furnaces, standardise measurements and share the cost of protecting supply routes. The agreement eventually became a military institution.
Its authority rests on the foundry ledger. Every weapon, shield, armour plate and Siege Engine component has a production record. Every soldier receives equipment according to a standard issue. A damaged breastplate is not a personal tragedy in the ledger. It is a material loss with a replacement cost.
The Compact's soldiers are built around the same philosophy. Their equipment is heavy, standardised and frequently ugly. Black iron armour is reinforced with thick rivets and squared plates. Furnace-red cloth identifies units. Bone-white markings indicate production batches and equipment categories.
Their workshops are hot, poorly ventilated and perpetually noisy. Workers breathe soot from childhood. Many have blackened nostrils, damaged teeth and burns along their forearms. A foundry worker who loses two fingers may still operate a furnace. A worker who loses the ability to measure or lift is assigned to cleaning slag pits.
Recruitment is tied to industrial obligation. Settlements supplying ore or fuel may be required to provide labour levies. A family can discharge part of its obligation by providing metal, but the Compact's accountants decide how much that metal is worth.
The Compact does not waste its dead. Bodies are removed from the battlefield when possible because armour, buckles and weapons must be recovered. In some foundry settlements, unidentified corpses are burned in industrial furnaces after useful equipment has been removed. Their ashes are mixed with slag and discarded.
The smell of a Compact encampment is unmistakable: hot iron, coal smoke, rancid grease, damp wool and burned hair.
Its Mages use Architect-derived mechanisms to amplify Mana. The instruments are maintained by foundry technicians, who treat magical components as another class of machinery. Aether and Ember production are measured in output, temperature and failure rate.
The Compact has no patience for romantic craftsmanship. A beautiful sword that cannot be reproduced is an unreliable weapon.
Its central doctrine is not that everything can be replaced. It is that everything must be accounted for, because anything unaccounted for eventually becomes somebody else's problem.

VIII. QUESTIONS FOR LORE v3
These questions concern the foundations of the world and the physical production of the game, rather than minor flavour details.
1. The Exile and the Architects
How direct should the relationship be between the Architects' towers and the Dragons' exile?
    A. The Architects accidentally pierced or disrupted a boundary separating Aurelia from the Sacred Place.
    B. The Architects deliberately constructed towers to reach or exploit something beyond the heavens, with consequences they could not understand.
    C. The towers and the Exile are demonstrably connected, but the precise mechanism is lost.
    D. Even the connection is disputed; the apparent relationship is based on chronology and surviving evidence.
My recommendation is C. It preserves a causal historical foundation without making the Architects omniscient or giving the Dragons a convenient explanation.
2. What does a Dragon physically look like?
We have established the two-legged wyvern anatomy and celestial origin. But should Dragons look:
    A. Almost divine: elegant, symmetrical, luminous, but visibly exhausted.
    B. Fallen and deteriorated: cracked scales, damaged wings, exposed anatomy, exhausted posture.
    C. Both: fundamentally majestic anatomy corrupted by centuries of exhaustion, with their original beauty still visible.
This will determine whether the miniatures look like fallen celestial beings or simply large monsters.
3. What happens to a human body during Spirit gathering?
We know the cost is bodily and spiritual, but should the manifestations be identical for Monks and Priests?
For example, Monks might experience progressive physical deformation, while Priests suffer neurological and sensory damage through repeated rites.
This would make the two Spirit-gathering traditions visually and narratively distinct without introducing different rules.
4. How much of the Architect civilisation survives physically?
Should Aurelia contain:
    A. Mostly dismantled ruins, with very few functioning Architect structures.
    B. Numerous abandoned mechanisms, some still operating without supervision.
    C. Entire surviving underground and enclosed complexes, with the surface civilisation built from their remains.
This affects the Mage designs, tower construction, resource-node appearance and environmental storytelling.
5. How should the eight institutions relate to one another?
Should their historical origins be intertwined?
For example, did the Chapter of the Last Nave and the Iron Fists once cooperate in defending Spirit-bearing territories, or have they always been separate institutions?
I particularly want to establish whether the institutions share a common historical foundation or whether each emerged independently from a different surviving part of the Architect civilisation.
