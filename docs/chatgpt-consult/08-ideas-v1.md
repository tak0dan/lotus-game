IDEAS v1 — The Tower Game
Design premise: The rules should be deterministic, but their interactions should not necessarily be obvious. A player who reads carefully, anticipates the resolution sequence, and understands the wording should be able to discover advantages that another player might overlook.
I would distinguish three kinds of intentional gaps:
    Literal loopholes: The rules permit something that appears unintended.
    System interactions: Two individually ordinary rules produce an unusual result when combined.
    Strategic ambiguities: The rules leave room for interpretation, timing advantages, or competing priorities.
The objective is not to make every interaction balanced. It is to make discovering and exploiting them part of the game.
1. Deliberate rule gaps and hidden interactions
Twelve possible interactions.

1
The Attached Tower Paradox
An attached tower is treated as part of the parent tower for some purposes, but as an independent structure for others.
Build an attached tower, then use rules concerning independent structures to create an unexpected defensive or territorial advantage.
Destroy the parent tower through siege, forcing the attacker to deal with the attached structure's independent status.
2
Arcane Bankruptcy
Arcane conversion changes the type of a resource, but the rules do not explicitly define whether conversion occurs before or after upkeep.
Convert resources immediately before upkeep to avoid an otherwise unavoidable economic failure. Alternatively, exploit the reverse timing interpretation.
Opponents can anticipate the conversion and attack the source of the resource before the conversion phase.
3
The Dragon's Second Landing
Dragons can land on towers, but the rules distinguish between occupying a tower and occupying a square.
Land on an attached tower, then use the distinction to interact with the parent tower's garrison or defensive rules.
Force the Dragon to leave through surrounding territory control or a concentrated attack.
4
Priest Ward Ownership
A Priest ward protects an area, but its wording does not explicitly establish whether protection follows the Priest, the protected unit, or the original square.
Place a ward, then move the Priest away. If the ward is square-bound, the protection remains behind. If unit-bound, it travels with the protected unit.
Attack the ward's origin or force movement through unprotected terrain.
5
The Monk's Return Clause
A Monk strikes and returns to its original position. What happens if that position becomes occupied during the resolution?
Deliberately arrange for another unit to occupy the return square. The Monk may become stranded, displaced, or force an unexpected interaction.
Reserve the return square or attack the Monk before its strike resolves.
6
Heavy Void Transit
Heavy units cannot enter certain terrain, but the rules may not explicitly prohibit being transported, displaced, or otherwise placed there.
Use a Dragon landing, tower interaction, or other movement effect to place a Heavy unit in normally inaccessible terrain.
Control the destination squares and prevent the enabling unit from reaching them.
7
The Last Card Doctrine
Orders consume cards from a yearly budget. Unused cards disappear, and there is no stockpile.
Submit a seemingly wasteful order to consume a card that would otherwise be lost, potentially triggering a secondary effect or denying an opponent a predictable interaction.
Force the player to commit their budget before they know the outcome of other orders.
8
Poverty as a Weapon
Upkeep can create a financial trap. A player who cannot maintain their forces faces disbandment or other consequences.
Deliberately over-recruit, creating a force that can be used for one decisive offensive year before upkeep collapses the economy.
Avoid fighting on the attacker's preferred timetable. Force them into a prolonged campaign.
9
Voluntary Disbandment
A player can disband units, but the rules do not necessarily specify whether disbandment happens before or after all other orders.
Disband expensive units to escape upkeep, clear occupied squares, or potentially deny an opponent a capture.
Surround important territory and exploit the resulting gaps in the player's defence.
10
The Terrain Boundary Exploit
Terrain determines movement, but the boundary between two terrain types may create special movement interactions.
Use a unit's movement allowance to approach a forbidden terrain boundary, then exploit a different rule that permits entering or leaving it.
Establish control over terrain transitions and use units with fewer movement restrictions.
11
The Empty Tower Siege
Towers can only be destroyed through siege, but the rules may not require a tower to have a garrison to be considered besieged.
Besiege an empty tower, tying up an opponent's defensive resources or destroying an important structure without fighting its garrison.
Maintain a mobile relief force and interrupt the siege before it resolves.
12
The Ghost of the Previous Year
Units heal fully at year-end, while orders are submitted simultaneously. The rules may not specify whether a unit that survives a strike but is subsequently moved retains its status for all later orders.
Time attacks and movements so that a damaged unit survives the resolution sequence and recovers before another engagement.
Concentrate attacks within the same year, reaching the required number of hits before healing occurs.
Particularly interesting interactions
I would prioritize three for further development:
A. The Monk's Return Clause
This is excellent because it creates a tactical puzzle without introducing randomness. A player can use another unit to manipulate the Monk's destination.
B. Poverty as a Weapon
This introduces a genuinely interesting economic decision: a player might intentionally sacrifice their long-term economy to win a short-term military objective.
C. The Dragon's Second Landing
Dragons should interact with the board in ways that ordinary units cannot. Exploiting differences between a tower, an attached tower, and a square could make Dragons strategically distinctive.
One important distinction: these are proposed loopholes, not established rules. Their exact exploitation depends on the eventual wording of the relevant mechanics.
2. Making 3–8 player games enjoyable and fair
The biggest problem with multiplayer strategy games isn't necessarily balance. It's player elimination, kingmaking, and downtime.
Eight players can create an extraordinary political game, but they can also create an extraordinary amount of waiting.
Eight structural ideas
1. The Vacant Throne
Strong recommendation
When a player is eliminated, their faction doesn't simply disappear.
Instead:
    Their towers remain on the board.
    Their garrisons become neutral defenders.
    Their resources and remaining structures follow a predefined abandonment rule.
    Their territory becomes available for occupation.
This creates strategic consequences for eliminating someone without leaving an enormous empty hole in the game.
An alternative: the eliminated player becomes the Keeper of the Ruins, controlling a limited number of neutral defensive structures.
This preserves their involvement without giving them unrestricted power.
2. The Last Testament
An eliminated player receives one final secret order.
Possible choices:
    Destroy one of their own structures.
    Transfer one tower to a designated successor.
    Leave a defensive garrison behind.
    Declare a territory neutral.
    Grant a one-time favour to another player.
The order resolves after their elimination.
This gives the eliminated player agency, but it should be limited enough that they cannot decide the winner through one spiteful action.
3. The Balance of Crowns
A player who becomes overwhelmingly powerful attracts the attention of other factions.
Introduce a public measure of power based on controlled territory, fortresses, and military presence.
Players may receive political incentives for opposing the current leader.
For example:
    Bonus victory points for capturing the leading faction's territory.
    Additional diplomatic privileges against the strongest faction.
    Special objectives for breaking a dominant player's siege.
The important part: the bonus should reward opposition, not directly punish success.
Otherwise, winning becomes a liability.
4. The Silent Treaty
Diplomacy exists outside the actual order system.
Players can:
    Negotiate non-aggression agreements.
    Exchange promises of territorial access.
    Declare joint military objectives.
    Make public declarations of war.
    Exchange information about intentions.
However, agreements are not mechanically binding unless a specific scenario says otherwise.
A broken treaty is therefore a legitimate strategic decision.
This fits Aurelia particularly well: honour is valuable precisely because betrayal is possible.
5. The Unclaimed Crown
Introduce neutral objectives across the board.
Examples:
    Ancient cathedrals.
    Abandoned royal capitals.
    Buried archives.
    Ancient Dragon nests.
    Ruined trade routes.
These objectives provide points or tactical advantages.
They give players something to fight over besides each other, reducing the likelihood that two neighbouring factions immediately eliminate one another.
6. The Ashen Succession
A defeated faction can return under a new political identity.
When eliminated, a player may eventually receive a small successor force emerging from a designated ruin or neutral settlement.
Restrictions:
    No inherited fortress.
    Limited initial resources.
    No immediate access to their former army.
    A defined return condition.
The returning player becomes a new political actor rather than simply recovering their previous position.
This is particularly suitable for campaign play.
7. Modular Political Geography
Strong recommendation
A large board should not be treated as one enormous continuous playing surface.
Instead, divide it into geographical regions.
For example:
Maladum - Game Overview - Battle Systems
    Each region consists of a modular tile.
    Tiles have consistent square-grid dimensions.
    Regional borders are clearly marked.
    Individual tiles can be rearranged between scenarios.
    Important locations are represented by physical terrain pieces.
This makes the 560×560 board physically manageable.
It also allows scenarios to use only a portion of Aurelia.
8. Asymmetric Victory Conditions
Not every player needs to pursue exactly the same victory objective.
Possible objectives:
    Hold an enemy fortress for a full year.
    Maintain control of several ancient towers.
    Accumulate a points majority for two consecutive years.
    Eliminate all rival factions.
    Control the central Spire.
    Prevent any faction from controlling the Spire until a particular year.
The trick is to make objectives visible to everyone.
Players should understand what others are trying to achieve, even if they cannot see their orders.
This creates political tension without relying on hidden victory conditions that suddenly invalidate an entire game.

6. The Council of Broken Crowns
Scenario type: Political campaign

Campaign progression idea
Scenarios could form a connected sequence:
    The March of Ash — establish territory.
    The Drowned Kingdoms — secure resources.
    The Hunt of the First Dragon — compete for ancient power.
    The Race to the Spire — construct a strategic centre.
    The Last Fortress — resolve the conflict.
Campaign outcomes determine starting resources, surviving structures, and available units in subsequent scenarios.
Importantly, I would avoid carrying over every individual unit. That could create an increasingly insurmountable advantage for whoever wins the earliest scenarios.

5. Five questions for IDEAS v2
These are the questions I consider most important before developing the next batch.
1. What exactly constitutes a tower's physical footprint?
Is a tower always one square, regardless of height, or does constructing additional levels change its footprint? What about attached towers?
This affects almost everything: movement, siege, garrison capacity, and board readability.
2. How much agency should eliminated players retain?
Should elimination mean:
    Complete removal from the game?
    Controlling neutral remnants?
    Becoming a limited secondary faction?
    Returning through succession?
This is probably the most important multiplayer design decision.
3. Are resources physically represented as cards, tokens, or something else?
The yearly budget system is interesting, but its physical implementation determines how easy it is to track eight simultaneous economies.
4. Is the Dragon an ordinary military unit, a strategic asset, or something closer to a mobile fortress?
This distinction determines whether Dragon interactions should be governed by ordinary combat rules or by a separate set of exceptional rules.
5. How much of the game should be determined by the resolution sequence versus the written order itself?
For example, if a player orders a unit to move into a square that becomes occupied during the same year, should:
    The movement fail?
    The unit stop at the last available square?
    The unit displace another unit?
    The order be interpreted according to a predetermined priority?
This is especially important in a simultaneous-order game. It determines whether clever planning is rewarded or whether players spend their time arguing over interpretations.

One overarching recommendation: Don't rush to close the loopholes. First establish a catalogue of them, including the exact wording that makes each possible. Some of the most memorable strategies in a deterministic game could emerge from interactions that look like mistakes in the rulebook.
The next step should be to distinguish intentional exploits from genuinely undefined resolution cases. The former create mastery; the latter can create arguments that last longer than the siege itself.
