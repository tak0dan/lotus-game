AURELIA — THE MILITARY CODEX

Volume II: The Instruments of War

The Fall did not leave Aurelia without armies. It left it without the institutions that once made armies necessary. The imperial legions dissolved, the great academies were burned or abandoned, and the religious orders were scattered across territories that no longer recognised their authority. What survived was not a unified military tradition, but fragments: hereditary companies, fortified schools, mercenary brotherhoods, provincial militias and institutions that had forgotten the purpose of their founding but retained every method of enforcing obedience.

The dismantling of the cathedrals changed warfare as profoundly as the Fall itself. Their iron became armour, their bells became siege counterweights, their reliquaries were emptied to supply alchemists, and their foundations became the sites of new fortifications. The old distinction between sacred and military architecture ceased to exist. A monastery could be a barracks, a barracks could be a prison, and a fortress could contain the remains of a god that nobody remembered how to worship.

The following entries describe the Aurelian institutions and military traditions as they actually exist. Their names are not merely battlefield classifications: they represent different answers to the same question — how does one preserve power in a world that has already demonstrated that every civilisation can die?
I. INFANTRY
Conventional archetype

Ordinary foot soldiers armed with swords, spears or shields, forming the backbone of medieval armies and holding the battlefield through numbers and discipline.
The Aurelian version

The Infantry of Aurelia are not exclusively soldiers. They are the foundation-builders, the conscripted labourers and the first inhabitants of every territory claimed by a surviving kingdom. Their recruitment begins with a census conducted by military officials, who record every able-bodied person in a settlement and assign them a value according to age, physical condition and useful skills. Those selected are given a weapon, a length of rope, a digging implement and a place in a formation. Their military education is deliberately elementary: hold your position, follow the standard, obey the officer and do not abandon the ground entrusted to you.

Their construction duties are inseparable from their military service. Infantry detachments establish tower foundations, dig defensive trenches, raise palisades and prepare the ground for permanent fortifications. A newly conquered territory is not considered occupied until its first foundation has been laid and a garrison assigned to it. The work is conducted under the supervision of military engineers, but the labour belongs to the soldiers themselves. Their hands are permanently scarred by stone, rope and iron tools; their boots are stained with lime and grave soil. In many territories, a soldier who survives long enough to complete a fortification is permitted to carve his name into its foundation stone. The names are usually buried beneath the next layer of masonry.
Schools, orders and branches

The Crown Levies — Provincial conscripts organised into standardised formations; their doctrine is to maintain the line regardless of casualties. Miniature detail: mismatched helmets bearing the same numbered iron identification plate.

The Foundation Companies — Military construction detachments responsible for establishing the first defensive works in conquered territory; they consider every wall an extension of the army. Miniature detail: heavy belts carrying measuring cords, masonry hammers and folded foundation plans.

The Ashen Tithe — Penal formations raised from settlements that failed to meet their military obligations; their service is considered repayment of an inherited debt. Miniature detail: grey execution collars and blackened hands permanently stained by mortar.
What others misunderstand

Foreign commanders often consider Infantry expendable because they are the least protected soldiers in the army. They overlook that Infantry are also the only formations capable of turning an empty territory into a defensible military possession. Killing them may win a battle; failing to account for their construction work may lose an entire campaign.
Motto

"First the blood. Then the stone. Then the kingdom."
Counter and weakness

Infantry possess no exceptional protection and are vulnerable to concentrated attacks. Their greatest weakness is their dependence on an established chain of command: once their officers, standards and supply arrangements are destroyed, their formations quickly lose cohesion.
II. HEAVY INFANTRY
Conventional archetype

Heavily armoured foot soldiers who withstand punishment, protect weaker formations and break enemy lines through superior protection and strength.
The Aurelian version

Heavy Infantry are the descendants of military households that preserved the techniques of the old imperial armouries. Their equipment is assembled from cathedral iron, salvaged imperial plate and components recovered from battlefields where entire companies disappeared. Their armour is not necessarily beautiful or uniform, but it is maintained according to strict technical standards. Every plate is inspected, every fastening tested, and every damaged section recorded in a register maintained by the company's armourers. Their bodies carry the consequences of wearing it: compressed joints, permanently damaged spines, scarred shoulders and fingers deformed by years of carrying excessive weight.

Their defining doctrine is the refusal to surrender ground to ordinary infantry. The armour is constructed with overlapping plates, reinforced joints and layered impact protection intended to absorb the blows of common weapons. A Heavy Infantry soldier can survive an otherwise fatal strike from ordinary Infantry or an attack delivered from a distance, provided the protection has not already been compromised. This is not immortality. The armour must be repaired, the soldier must be fed, and the man inside remains vulnerable to exhaustion, disease and wounds that accumulate beyond the capacity of his equipment. Before battle, each soldier undergoes the Iron Examination: armourers strike the plates with a calibrated hammer while the wearer stands motionless. A defective plate means removal from the formation. A defective soldier is usually given a less demanding assignment until his body fails completely.
Schools, orders and branches

The Cathedral Remnants — Successors to the old imperial heavy legions, committed to preserving the armour-making traditions of the pre-Fall armies. Miniature detail: massive mismatched cathedral plates bearing fragments of carved religious inscriptions.

The Black Anvils — Defensive formations trained to absorb repeated infantry assaults without yielding ground. Miniature detail: oversized shoulder plates with visible rivets and blacksmith marks.

The Oathbound — Military households whose armour is inherited, repaired and passed down through generations, regardless of the identity of its wearer. Miniature detail: family names and casualty tallies engraved inside the breastplate, visible through open armour.
What others misunderstand

Their protection is not simply a consequence of wearing thicker armour. Their equipment is designed as a system in which individual plates, joints and supporting layers distribute the force of an impact. Their resistance is therefore not reproduced by simply forging a heavier breastplate.
Motto

"The ground beneath our feet is not surrendered."
Counter and weakness

Their protection is specialised rather than universal. It is effective against ordinary Infantry and attacks from a distance, but it does not negate specialised heavy-cavalry impacts or the destructive effects of other exceptional weapons. Their weight also makes prolonged movement and rapid redeployment costly.
III. CAVALRY
Conventional archetype

Mounted soldiers who use speed, manoeuvrability and charging attacks to outflank enemies and exploit weaknesses in battlefield formations.
The Aurelian version

Cavalry in Aurelia are organised around the surviving imperial road network, whose routes still determine the movement of armies across much of the continent. Their horses are bred in specialised estates, where bloodlines are recorded more carefully than those of the soldiers who ride them. A mount is trained from birth to tolerate the sounds of battle, the smell of burning flesh and the pressure of formations moving around it. Its hooves are reinforced with specially forged shoes, and its feed is rationed according to campaign distance. The death of a trained warhorse is entered into the military ledger as a material loss, while the death of its rider is recorded separately.

The Cavalry are particularly valuable in a world where fortifications have replaced much of the open infrastructure of the old empire. They are trained to cover long distances, strike exposed formations and exploit gaps before an opposing commander can reorganise his forces. Their advantage is not the ability to defeat every enemy in direct combat, but the ability to choose where and when an engagement occurs. They cannot climb towers, and the increasing density of fortified settlements has gradually reduced the territory in which they can operate freely. Their officers therefore maintain detailed records of roads, bridges, grazing grounds and seasonal flooding. A cavalry commander who loses his horses to hunger is considered to have failed before the enemy has even appeared.
Schools, orders and branches

The Imperial Road Wardens — Successors to the mounted couriers and patrols of the old empire, specialising in rapid movement between settlements. Miniature detail: long travel cloaks, iron road tokens and narrow helmets with horsehair crests.

The Salt Lancers — Mounted formations recruited from the dry southern territories, trained to endure long campaigns with limited provisions. Miniature detail: pale leather tack, salt-stained caparisons and long, narrow lances.

The Ash Riders — Mercenary cavalry who specialise in pursuing retreating formations and raiding supply columns. Miniature detail: darkened armour, trophies tied to saddle straps and horses with partially shaven manes.
What others misunderstand

The Cavalry are not primarily a shock force. Their greatest contribution is the ability to make an opponent defend several places simultaneously. An enemy who sees mounted banners on the horizon must decide whether to protect his supply lines, reinforce his flanks or remain concentrated around his towers. That decision is often more valuable than the charge itself.
Motto

"Where the road ends, the dead begin."
Counter and weakness

Cavalry cannot climb towers and cannot replace a properly equipped siege force. Fortified terrain, restricted passages and the loss of suitable mounts severely limit their usefulness.
IV. HEAVY CAVALRY
Conventional archetype

Armoured mounted knights who combine the speed of cavalry with heavy protection and devastating charges.
The Aurelian version

Heavy Cavalry are maintained by the wealthiest surviving military institutions, since their horses, armour and specialised equipment demand resources beyond the means of ordinary settlements. The animals are selected from a small number of carefully preserved bloodlines and undergo years of conditioning before they are permitted to carry a fully armoured rider. Their armour is constructed in separate sections for horse and rider, joined by reinforced fittings that distribute the force of an impact without restricting movement. The resulting formations are expensive, difficult to maintain and politically significant. A kingdom that can field Heavy Cavalry demonstrates that it possesses more than soldiers: it possesses the industrial capacity to sustain them.

Their battlefield doctrine is based on controlled momentum and the ability to survive the first exchange of blows. Like Heavy Infantry, their protection can void one otherwise effective hit per year, but their specialised armour is designed to resist the attacks of ordinary Cavalry. This resistance does not make them invulnerable to every threat; it makes them exceptionally difficult to destroy through conventional mounted warfare. Before a campaign, the mounts are fitted with their armour in a ceremony known as the Weighing, during which the armourers assess every strap, rivet and plate. The horse is not permitted to carry the full load until it has completed a prescribed circuit without stumbling. Those that fail are removed from the breeding programme. Those that pass are branded with the insignia of their formation.
Schools, orders and branches

The Gilded Spurs — Aristocratic cavalry households that preserve the ceremonial and military traditions of the pre-Fall nobility. Miniature detail: ornate but tarnished gilded barding, with family crests hammered into the horse's chest plate.

The Iron Procession — Heavy formations trained to maintain a single advancing line, using their armour and mass to resist enemy cavalry. Miniature detail: identical enclosed helmets, rectangular horse armour and black standards.

The Sepulchral Guard — Mounted protectors of dynastic tombs and ancestral territories, whose members inherit their positions along with their equipment. Miniature detail: funeral cloths draped over dark plate barding and small bone reliquaries attached to saddles.
What others misunderstand

Their resilience is not merely a consequence of individual armour quality. The rider, horse and equipment are treated as a single military system. A rider who survives while his mount is crippled has not preserved the formation's combat capability. Heavy Cavalry are trained and equipped to prevent that separation.
Motto

"Our weight is your judgement."
Counter and weakness

Their special protection applies against ordinary Cavalry, not against every weapon on the battlefield. Their maintenance costs are enormous, and the loss of trained mounts cannot be compensated for quickly. They are also restricted by the same inability to climb towers as ordinary Cavalry.
V. ARCHERS
Conventional archetype

Ranged infantry who use bows or crossbows to weaken enemy formations from a distance, usually relying on positioning and elevation.
The Aurelian version

The Archers of Aurelia descend from the hereditary bow companies that once guarded the imperial roads, hunting estates and fortified settlements. Their profession survived the Fall because the bow requires comparatively little industrial infrastructure and can be maintained even where the great armouries have disappeared. Their training begins with the manufacture of their own equipment. An apprentice must learn to select suitable timber, prepare bowstrings, straighten shafts and forge arrowheads before being permitted to carry a military bow. A bowman who cannot repair his weapon is considered a liability to his formation. Their hands are thick with calluses, their fingertips wrapped in leather, and their forearms permanently discoloured by years of string contact.

The greatest distinction of Aurelian Archers is their ability to engage garrisons sheltered inside towers. Their formations train specifically to exploit openings, elevated firing angles and exposed architectural features, delivering volleys into positions that ordinary infantry cannot reach. Tower warfare has transformed archery from a supporting discipline into an indispensable part of siege doctrine. Every fortified settlement must account for the possibility that its defenders will be killed without the walls ever being breached. Archers are taught to count their ammunition by the dozen, not the individual arrow, and every company maintains a register of recovered shafts. In some schools, an arrow returned from the battlefield is inspected for damage before its head is removed and reforged. Waste is treated as a military offence.
Schools, orders and branches

The Bell-Tower Companies — Specialists in elevated firing positions and engagements against fortified garrisons. Miniature detail: tall-backed quivers, narrow iron sighting frames and scraps of bell-rope tied around their wrists.

The Pale Fletchers — Hereditary bowmakers and marksmen who consider the manufacture of ammunition inseparable from the act of killing. Miniature detail: pale feathered arrows, leather tool rolls and bone measuring gauges.

The Gallows Watch — Execution-ground bowmen employed to suppress rebellions and punish settlements that refuse military taxation. Miniature detail: black hoods, hanging nooses worn as insignia and unusually short bows.
What others misunderstand

Archers are often considered incapable of influencing a fortress without first breaching its walls. Their training is specifically designed around the opposite principle: a tower protects its occupants from approaching soldiers, but it cannot guarantee that those occupants are beyond the reach of a carefully organised firing formation.
Motto

"Stone conceals the living. It does not silence the arrow."
Counter and weakness

Archers depend on ammunition, visibility and suitable firing positions. Their formations are vulnerable to fast Cavalry, Heavy Cavalry and direct assaults. They are not siege engines and cannot destroy tower structures.
VI. PRIESTS
Conventional archetype

Religious healers and divine spellcasters who restore the wounded, bless soldiers and protect armies through sacred magic.
The Aurelian version

The Priests of Aurelia are the surviving custodians of spiritual rites that existed before the Fall and outlived the destruction of the institutions that once administered them. They do not form a single unified church. Their authority is divided among religious houses, wandering fraternities and territorial cults, many of which disagree on the identity of the powers they serve. Some preserve fragments of the old imperial liturgy; others claim descent from religious institutions dismantled during the Architects' dominion. Their vestments are made from whatever ceremonial fabrics survived the destruction of the cathedrals, frequently reinforced with chain, iron thread and protective inscriptions. Their faces are often marked with ritual incisions, ash and pigments that identify their order and rank.

Their battlefield function is neither conventional healing nor the restoration of the dead. A Priest establishes protective wards around nearby allies, extending spiritual protection to every soldier within two squares. Each protected ally can withstand one otherwise fatal hit during the year. This protection is not a physical barrier in the ordinary sense; it is a temporary alteration of the relationship between the protected individual and the destructive forces acting upon them. The Priest must remain conscious and maintain the rite, drawing upon the spiritual resources that sustain the ward. The same concentration allows Priests to gather Spirit resources, which they collect through ritual, invocation and the controlled manipulation of spiritual remnants. Their hands tremble after prolonged ceremonies, their throats bleed from sustained chanting, and their vestments retain the smell of burned incense mixed with old blood. A Priest who protects an entire formation is not merely offering a blessing. He is accepting the burden of maintaining a spiritual structure whose collapse can condemn everyone beneath it.
Schools, orders and branches

The Ashen Litany — A militant priesthood that treats protection as an obligation imposed upon the living rather than a gift from the divine. Miniature detail: blackened ceremonial masks, iron prayer chains and ash-filled censers.

The Keepers of the Last Vow — Custodians of defensive rites preserved from the old cathedrals, specialising in maintaining protective wards over military formations. Miniature detail: heavy wooden prayer tablets, layered vestments and small iron bells attached to their staffs.

The Silent Communion — A secretive order that collects spiritual remnants from battlefields and regards death as a passage into a greater spiritual reservoir. Miniature detail: sealed reliquaries, pale veils and small glass vessels containing darkened fragments.
What others misunderstand

Many soldiers believe the Priest's power comes from the strength of his faith or the benevolence of the entity he worships. In reality, the wards are established spiritual structures with identifiable limits and requirements. A Priest's personal beliefs may determine how he interprets the rite, but the rite itself does not require the universe to approve of him. The same protection can be invoked by priests whose religions consider one another heretical.
Motto

"You shall not pass into death while my hands remain raised."
Counter and weakness

A Priest's protection is limited to allies within two squares and each ally can benefit from only one voided hit per year. The Priest himself remains vulnerable. Breaking his concentration, removing him from the formation or overwhelming the ward with successive attacks can leave the protected soldiers exposed.
VII. SIEGE ENGINES
Conventional archetype

Large mechanical weapons such as trebuchets, catapults and battering rams, designed to destroy fortifications and support the assault of fortified positions.
The Aurelian version

The Siege Engines are the most direct inheritors of the industrial remains of the Architects of Silence. Their construction draws upon dismantled cathedral iron, abandoned imperial machinery and engineering principles preserved in fragments of technical manuscripts. Their designs vary considerably between territories, but their purpose remains unchanged: to destroy tower structures that ordinary armies cannot bring down. The largest machines require entire companies of craftsmen, labourers and military engineers to assemble, transport and maintain. Their components are manufactured separately, often in distant workshops, before being brought together at the site of an intended campaign. The resulting machines are enormous, ugly constructions of timber, iron, counterweights and tensioned mechanisms. Their crews work beneath a constant layer of grease, soot and metallic dust.

A Siege Engine is not simply another weapon. It is the only instrument in Aurelia's military tradition capable of breaking a tower structure. Its operation is therefore governed by strict military procedures, and its deployment is treated as a strategic commitment rather than a routine battlefield manoeuvre. The crews are organised into specialised ranks: operators, tensioners, loaders, structural inspectors and engineers responsible for measuring the condition of the machine after each engagement. The destruction of a tower is recorded in a formal register, including the number of impacts required, the condition of the machine and the estimated quantity of material lost. Some crews maintain small collections of stones taken from the towers they have destroyed. Others refuse to touch the remains, believing that the Architects embedded something within their foundations that should never be disturbed. Neither superstition nor professional caution has prevented the machines from being built.
Schools, orders and branches

The Iron Choir — Imperial-descended engineering companies whose machines follow standardised construction patterns and require strict crew coordination. Miniature detail: multiple iron counterweights bearing stamped serial numbers, with crew members wearing identical protective masks.

The Cathedral Breakers — Specialist siege companies that salvage architectural iron and masonry from conquered territories to construct and maintain their engines. Miniature detail: enormous wooden beams reinforced with visibly repurposed cathedral ornaments.

The Black Calculators — Independent engineers who preserve mathematical and mechanical manuscripts from the pre-Fall period. Miniature detail: measuring instruments, engraved calculation plates and scrolls attached to the machine's frame.
What others misunderstand

The Siege Engines are often mistaken for weapons of indiscriminate destruction. Their operators understand that their true purpose is structural termination. They are not intended to kill the soldiers defending a tower; they are intended to eliminate the tower itself. A garrison may survive a battle, but it cannot remain protected once its fortification has ceased to exist.
Motto

"Every wall has a final measurement."
Counter and weakness

Siege Engines are expensive, slow to reposition and dependent on specialist crews. They require protection from mobile formations and are vulnerable when caught without a defensive screen. Their destructive capacity does not compensate for their inability to defend themselves.
VIII. MAGES
Conventional archetype

Arcane scholars who manipulate magical forces, cast destructive spells and harness supernatural energies to support their armies.
The Aurelian version

The Mages of Aurelia are the surviving practitioners of an intellectual tradition that was already fragmented before the Fall. Some descend from imperial academies, others from the clandestine schools established by exiled alchemists, and many have inherited their knowledge through isolated families and private collections of manuscripts. Their education is long, expensive and frequently fatal. An apprentice is expected to learn the properties of Mana, the structure of its deposits and the consequences of uncontrolled extraction before being permitted to conduct an independent experiment. The surviving texts are incomplete, and many of the most important principles are known only through practical demonstrations preserved by individual schools. Their laboratories smell of heated mineral dust, burned parchment and chemical solvents. Their hands are often stained by substances that cannot be washed away.

The Mages' most valuable battlefield contribution is not necessarily the destruction they can inflict, but their ability to increase the yield of Mana deposits. By establishing a controlled connection between themselves and a nearby resource node, a Mage doubles the quantity of Mana that can be extracted from it. This process requires specialised knowledge of the deposit's internal structure and the ability to maintain a stable relationship between the source and the practitioner's own arcane apparatus. The Mage does not create additional Mana; he unlocks a greater proportion of what is already present. This distinction is fundamental to their profession. The Architects of Silence once operated vast systems capable of extracting and processing arcane energy on an industrial scale. The Mages of Aurelia work with fragments of that infrastructure, often without fully understanding the principles upon which it was constructed. Every successful extraction is therefore both a professional achievement and a reminder of how much knowledge has been lost.
Schools, orders and branches

The Collegium of Ash — Successors to the imperial academies, committed to preserving formal arcane theory and standardising the extraction of Mana. Miniature detail: layered scholar's robes, metal instrument cases and circular measuring devices suspended from belts.

The Emberwrights — Practical arcane engineers who specialise in manipulating the destructive and volatile properties of Mana. Miniature detail: scorched gloves, sealed flasks and darkened metal rods bearing heat fractures.

The Aetheric Synod — A secretive scholarly institution dedicated to reconstructing the energy systems of the Architects of Silence. Miniature detail: intricate headpieces, suspended crystals and fragments of ancient architectural mechanisms.
What others misunderstand

Most people assume Mages create magical energy through their own knowledge and will. In reality, their greatest achievement is understanding how to access and regulate power that already exists. A Mage standing beside a Mana deposit is not a source of power. He is an intermediary between the world and forces that were present long before his birth.
Motto

"We do not create. We remember how to take."
Counter and weakness

Mages depend on accessible Mana deposits and the ability to maintain their connection to them. Their specialised knowledge does not make them inherently resistant to physical attacks, and their work can be disrupted by the destruction or exhaustion of the resource sources upon which they rely.
IX. DRAGONS
Conventional archetype

Enormous flying reptiles capable of breathing fire, devastating armies and claiming territories through overwhelming physical and magical power.
The Aurelian version

The Dragons of Aurelia are enormous winged predators, anatomically closer to monstrous wyverns than the four-legged dragons of ancient mythology. Their bodies are supported by powerful hind limbs and vast leathery wings, while their elongated necks and reinforced skulls are adapted for hunting large terrestrial prey. Their scales are dark, often resembling scorched iron or weathered stone, and their bodies bear the accumulated scars of territorial conflicts with other Dragons. They are not products of human alchemy, nor are they servants of the Architects of Silence. Their existence predates the surviving kingdoms, and their territories were already established when the first imperial roads were laid. The Fall did not create them. It merely removed much of the infrastructure that once kept their movements predictable.

Their military significance comes from their ability to perch upon tall towers and move between them without returning to the ground. A Dragon that settles upon a tower crushes the garrison beneath its weight, leaving the fortification itself standing but its occupants dead. The landing is not a conventional assault: the creature's mass, impact and territorial aggression make the tower's interior a death chamber. Its scales and musculature are exceptionally resilient, requiring five successful hits to kill one. Ordinary military formations cannot easily bring one down, and its ability to move between elevated positions allows it to threaten several fortifications within a single campaign. Arcane Towers are the sole exception. The concentrated spiritual and magical forces bound into their foundations prevent Dragons from perching upon them. The creatures appear to recognise this boundary instinctively, refusing to settle upon their summits even when pursued or wounded. Whether this is a consequence of their natural senses or an inherited response to the Architects' ancient works remains unknown.

Dragons are not domesticated in the conventional sense. Their lairs are established through territorial occupation, and the surrounding settlements survive through tribute, avoidance or simple geographical luck. Some communities maintain elaborate feeding arrangements, leaving livestock and prisoners beyond the settlements in the hope that the creature will not descend upon their homes. Others build observation towers whose sole purpose is to record the Dragon's movements and warn neighbouring territories. The appearance of one in a campaign changes the calculations of every commander involved. A kingdom may possess an army capable of defeating every neighbouring force and still lose its territory because a Dragon has chosen to make its towers hunting grounds.
Schools, orders and branches

These are not Dragon schools in the human sense, but distinct human institutions and practices associated with their presence.

The Scaled Tribute — Communities and subordinate rulers who maintain formal arrangements with territorial Dragons, providing livestock, metals and prisoners in exchange for temporary non-aggression. Miniature detail: tribute bearers carrying chained offerings, scales sewn into ceremonial cloaks and livestock skulls mounted on poles.

The Wyrmwatch — Military observation companies specialising in identifying Dragon movements and tracking their territorial boundaries. Miniature detail: tall signal horns, dark telescopes and banners bearing the silhouette of a spread-winged wyvern.

The Ashen Hunts — Specialist hunting expeditions formed by rulers who refuse to tolerate a Dragon's territorial occupation. Their campaigns consume enormous quantities of resources and rarely produce survivors. Miniature detail: oversized harpoons, scale fragments attached to armour and blackened hunting standards.
What others misunderstand

Most inhabitants believe Dragons attack towers because they seek defensible nesting sites or valuable treasures. Their behaviour is more territorial than acquisitive. A tower provides an elevated position from which a Dragon can survey its hunting grounds, establish dominance and monitor movement across the surrounding territory. It does not need the building itself, nor does it distinguish between the soldiers inside and the livestock outside. The garrison is simply an obstacle occupying a desirable perch.

The exception of Arcane Towers is particularly poorly understood. Dragons do not fear their occupants, and they do not recognise the political authority of their builders. They simply refuse to settle upon structures whose foundations carry the concentrated forces of Soul, Aether and Ember. Even a wounded Dragon will abandon an otherwise suitable perch rather than land upon one.
Motto

"The earth belongs to those who can hold it. The sky belongs to none."
Counter and weakness

Dragons are exceptionally difficult to kill, requiring five successful hits, and cannot be removed through ordinary battlefield tactics alone. They cannot perch upon Arcane Towers, making those structures the only reliable sanctuary for a garrison against their landing attacks. Their territorial behaviour can also be exploited: their movements follow the logic of hunting grounds rather than conventional military objectives.

A final principle for Aurelia's military lore

I would maintain one distinction throughout future codex entries:

The military of Aurelia is not a unified institution. It is an arrangement of institutions that happen to fight under the same banners.

An Infantryman might be a conscript who has never seen his homeland beyond its walls. A Heavy Cavalry rider might belong to a household whose armour is older than the kingdom employing him. A Priest might consider the Mage beside him a heretic, while both are perfectly willing to defend the same tower. A Monk might understand a spiritual phenomenon that neither of them can explain.

They cooperate because their interests temporarily coincide, not because they share a worldview.

That gives Aurelia room for political fractures, competing doctrines, military rivalries and regional variations without requiring a completely different civilisation for every army. It also reinforces the central historical condition of the setting: the Architects of Silence are gone, but the institutions, materials and consequences of their dominion remain embedded in practically everything that still functions.

The cathedrals are gone. Their iron marches. Their bells no longer ring. Their stones still remember.
